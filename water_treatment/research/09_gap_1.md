# Gap 1 — RO-side continuous chemical dosing: antiscalant dose, scaling kinetics, and biofouling control

**Scope.** The master report treats UF coagulant dosing and CIP chemistry in depth but leaves the RO train's *continuous* chemical levers unmodelled. Those levers are (i) antiscalant dose, (ii) acid/pH control, and (iii) biocide strategy. They matter for the optimizer, not just the chemical budget: every recovery-pushing recommendation in the playbook is bounded by a scaling or biofouling constraint that the report never writes down. File 01 says explicitly that LSI "is a thermodynamic tendency, not a rate — kinetics must be modelled or learned separately," and then never does. This file closes that loop: the thermodynamic constraint, the kinetic (induction-time) constraint that converts it into an *operable* rate limit, the published dose-search algorithms that beat vendor dose recommendations by 26–90 %, the equation-oriented formulations that put scaling tendency inside an NLP, and the biofouling side that no saturation index can see.

---

## 1. Why this is the binding constraint on the RTO prize

Fouling is not a rounding error in RO opex. Jafari, Vanoppen, van Agtmaal, Cornelissen, Vrouwenvelder, Verliefde, van Loosdrecht & Picioreanu (Desalination, 2021, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.desal.2020.114865) audited seven full-scale Dutch installations and found **fouling-related cost ≈ 24 % of OPEX in the RO plants and ≈ 11 % in NF**, dominated by early membrane replacement, then extra energy, then cleaning chemicals and cleaning downtime. Any recovery increase that shortens element life pays for itself out of the wrong pocket.

The thermodynamic ceiling moves fast with recovery. In the WaterTAP high-recovery study of Amusat, Dudchenko, Atia & Bartholomew (LBNL/SLAC/NETL, FOCAPD 2024, https://netl.doe.gov/projects/files/CostoptimalselectionpHcontrolmineralscalingpreventionhirecoveryreverseosmosisdesalination_071024.pdf, DOI 10.69997/sct.143335), LCOW is essentially flat with recovery until the gypsum constraint activates, then rises steeply: **softening onset at 64–66 % recovery for brackish (Na⁺ 739, Ca²⁺ 258, Mg²⁺ 90, SO₄²⁻ 1011, HCO₃⁻ 385 mg/L, pH 7.07) and 74 % for seawater** (Na⁺ 10 556, Ca²⁺ 400, Mg²⁺ 1262, SO₄²⁻ 2649, HCO₃⁻ 140 mg/L, pH 7.56), both at 5 000 m³/d. Below the knee the only chemical lever is acid; above it, calcium removal dominates the cost stack. An RTO that does not know where its plant's knee is will either leave recovery on the table or walk the plant into irreversible sulfate scale.

## 2. Thermodynamics: what to compute, and where

Define the saturation ratio and index for scalant $s$:

$$S_{a,s} = \frac{IAP_s}{K_{sp,s}}, \qquad SI_s = \log_{10} S_{a,s} \;\;(\text{or } \ln S_{a,s}, \text{ per Tong et al. 2025})$$

with $IAP = \prod_i (\gamma_i m_i)^{\nu_i}$ and activity coefficients $\gamma_i$ from Debye–Hückel/Pitzer. For CaCO₃ the practical proxies are LSI (`pH − pH_s`, ASTM D3739) below ~10 000 mg/L TDS and Stiff & Davis above; for CaSO₄·2H₂O, BaSO₄, SrSO₄ and silica you need an ion-association or Pitzer model — OLI, PHREEQC, or Reaktoro.

Two errors are structural and both bias the optimizer toward *unsafe* recovery:

1. **Compute on the concentrate, at the wall.** Bulk concentrate concentration understates the true driving force by the concentration-polarization modulus $\beta = C_w/C_b = \exp(J_w/k)$, with $k$ the film mass-transfer coefficient from a spacer-corrected Sherwood correlation. The overall concentration factor down a vessel at recovery $Y$ and rejection $R$ is $CF = [1 - Y(1-R)]/(1-Y)$; the wall value is $CF\cdot\beta$. Vrouwenvelder-adjacent surface work notes commercial polyamide roughness creates local flux hot-spots 3–6× the average with CP modulus 2–3× in spacer regions (npj Clean Water, 2022, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41545-022-00183-0) — i.e. the *local* wall SI exceeds the design value materially.
2. **Antiscalant does not lower saturation.** Mangal (PhD thesis, IHE Delft / U. Twente, 2023, https://ris.utwente.nl/ws/files/301011511/2023_IHE_THESIS_PHD_MANGAL_i.pdf) makes the point that vendor projection programs *display* a reduced saturation level with antiscalant, but "the actual saturation level of the scaling compounds would remain the same"; the inhibitor acts on nucleation and growth kinetics only. Modelling antiscalant as a $\Delta SI$ credit is physically wrong and will mis-locate the optimum.

The Reaktoro-in-WaterTAP work (Akkor, Amusat, Vecchiarelli, Gounaris, Knueven & Dudchenko, ACS ES&T Engineering, 2026, OSTI full text https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/3030749; code https://github.com/watertap-org/reaktoro-pse) does this properly: it evaluates scaling tendency using the apparent species entering the RO plus the equivalent water removed *at the membrane interface*,

$$\dot m^{removed}_{H_2O} = \dot m^{in}_{H_2O} - \dot m^{in}_{TDS}\frac{\dot m^{interface}_{H_2O}}{\dot m^{interface}_{TDS}}$$

and adds a monotonicity constraint forcing the last node to hold the highest TDS so scaling is evaluated where it actually occurs.

## 3. Kinetics: turning SI into an operable rate constraint

### 3.1 The classical linearization

Classical nucleation theory gives the homogeneous nucleation rate $J \propto \exp\!\left[-\frac{16\pi\gamma^3 v_m^2}{3(k_BT)^3(\ln S)^2}\right]$ and, on the assumption $t_{ind}\propto 1/J$, the workhorse regression used across the RO scaling literature:

$$\ln t_{ind} = A + \frac{B}{(\ln S_w)^2}, \qquad B = \frac{\beta_g\,\gamma^3 v_m^2}{(k_BT)^3}$$

Fit $A,B$ from jar tests or membrane runs and you have a per-water, per-scalant induction-time curve as a function of *wall* supersaturation $S_w$. This is the minimum viable kinetics model and it is what Hasson-type datasets support (see §3.2).

### 3.2 A closed-form induction-time model with and without antiscalant

The most implementable recent result is Sagiv, Semiat & Shemer (Technion, *Applied Sciences* 14:4700, 2024, https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/14/11/4700). They derive induction time from a mass/momentum balance on a growing cluster rather than from nucleation-rate proportionality, exploiting $Re \ll 1$ near the cluster so the Stokes field is exact.

Critical cluster size:

$$n_c = \frac{32\pi V_m^2\gamma^3}{3\,(k_BT\ln S_a)^3}, \qquad S_a = C_f/C_e$$

Cluster growth with a shear-removal term:

$$\frac{dm}{dt} = k_c A (C_f - C_e)^z - k_s F_s, \qquad F_s = \tfrac{3}{2}\pi^2\eta R u_d$$

Eliminating $u_d$ via the Stokes-drag/inertia balance $6\pi\eta R u_d = (u_f-u_d)\,dm/dt$ and using $u_d \ll u_f$:

$$\frac{dm}{dt} = \frac{4k_cA(C_f-C_e)^z}{4+\pi u_f k_s}$$

with $m(n)=(M_w/N_A)n$, $V(n)=nV_m/(0.635N_A)$ (random hard-sphere packing), $A(n)=A(1)n^{2/3}$, $A(1)=6.55(V_m/N_A)^{2/3}$. Integrating to $n=n_c$:

$$\boxed{\,t_{0A} = \frac{(4+\pi k_s u_f)M_w}{4k_cN_AA(1)(C_f-C_e)^z}\cdot 3n_c^{1/3}\,}$$

Antiscalant enters as competitive Langmuir-type site occupancy $\theta$ on the cluster surface:

$$\frac{d\theta}{dt} = k_aC_a(1-\theta)-k_d\theta \;\Rightarrow\; \theta(t)=\frac{a}{b}\left(1-e^{-bt}\right),\; a=k_aC_a,\; b=a+k_d$$

$$\frac{dm}{dt} = \frac{4k_cA(1-\theta)(C_f-C_e)^z}{4+\pi u_f k_s} \;\Rightarrow\; \boxed{\,t_A = \frac{1}{b-a}\left(b\,t_{0A} - \frac{a}{b}\left(1-e^{-b t_A}\right)\right)\,}$$

$t_A$ is implicit in itself — solve by fixed-point or Newton in one variable. $t_A \to t_{0A}$ as $C_a \to 0$, and $t_A$ is approximately linear in antiscalant concentration $C_a$. **This is the missing dose→time transfer function.**

Parameterization actually reported: $\gamma = 9.25$ mJ/m² for CaSO₄; $z=2$ (surface-reaction controlled); $k_c = 5.31\times10^{-8}$ kg/(s·m²·mM²). Fitted $k_s$: $1.73\times10^{4}$ s/m (spiral-wound, Hasson dataset), $2.43\times10^{5}$ s/m (spiral-wound, Li dataset), $7.37\times10^{4}$ s/m (tubular). Fitted antiscalant constants: $k_a = 3.17\times10^{-4}$ L/(mg·s), $k_d = 4.0\times10^{-4}$ s⁻¹ (phosphonic acid sodium salt, 6 mg/L); $k_a = 1.0\times10^{-3}$ L/(mg·s), $k_d = 6.5\times10^{-5}$ s⁻¹ (phosphinocarboxylic acid, 2 mg/L). Validation across six datasets gave average errors of **8.0 % ($t_{0A}$) and 8.7 % ($t_A$)** (per-system table: 10.8/10.7/2.4 % and 10.8/5.2/10.0 %, individual range 0.4–20.0 %).

The engineering payoff: **$k_s$ is the only system-specific fitting parameter** — it absorbs channel geometry, spacer type and local shear — while $k_a,k_d$ are properties of the *scalant–antiscalant pair* and transfer across systems. That is exactly the factorization you want for a plant model: characterize $k_s$ once per element/spacer type, buy $k_a,k_d$ per product.

Validation conditions worth reusing as a test harness: Hasson et al. spiral-wound TFC-ULP 2514, 15 bar, 475 L/h (0.72 m/s), 28–30 °C, Ca²⁺ 27–36 mM, SO₄²⁻ 12–16 mM, CaSO₄ $S_a$ 1.6–2.0, 6 mg/L inhibitor. Li et al. tubular AFC99 (2×30 cm, 12.5 mm ID), once-through, SI 3.3, 0.16–2.64 m/s, 30 bar, 22–24 °C; and Filmtec SW30-2521 (1.19 m²), full recycle, 30 bar, 0.07–0.20 m/s, gypsum SI 2.54, 2 mg/L AS.

### 3.3 Scalant-specific kinetics: gypsum ≠ silica ≠ calcite

Tong et al. (*Frontiers of Environmental Science & Engineering* 19(1):3, 2025, https://doi.org/10.1007/s11783-025-1923-9) quantify why one kinetic constraint cannot cover all scalants:

- **Gypsum** is crystallization-controlled and fast. Mi & Elimelech data: SI = 0.26 (natural-log convention) produced ~50 % flux decline in **1 500 min**; facet growth ~0.05 µm/min on the reactive [001] face at SI ≈ 0.64. Morphology is oriented/intrusive (rosettes on polyamide indicate surface-induced heterogeneous nucleation).
- **Silica** is polymerization-controlled and slow but effectively irreversible. Similar flux decline required **SI = 0.79**; in MD, saturated silica caused no visible decline for >1 000 min where gypsum at the same SI went to near-zero flux in 600 min. Brackish silica 12–60 mg/L against a solubility of **100–150 mg/L at near-neutral pH**; silicic acid pK$_a$ ≈ 9.8, and polymerization is **fastest at pH 8–9** (both ionized and neutral silicic acid abundant), slow at low pH and suppressed above pH ~11. Consequence: acid dosing for calcite control moves you *toward* the silica-worst pH window only if you are already alkaline — and in high-pH boron-rejection designs it is actively dangerous. Silica scale was not removable by physical cleaning where gypsum ~80 % flux recovery was achievable.
- **Calcite** responds primarily to pH. In the FOCAPD optimization the calcite constraint $\widehat{ST}_{calcite}=1.0$ was **active over the entire recovery range** — i.e. acid dose is always at its scaling-limited value, and pH is the calcite lever, while soda ash is the gypsum lever.
- **Calcium phosphate** breaks the antiscalant assumption entirely. Mangal (2023) found that above 80 % recovery on anaerobic groundwater containing 2.1 mg/L orthophosphate, amorphous calcium phosphate was the precipitating phase, and phosphonate antiscalants were *not effective* against it — the dose-search algorithm could no longer find an optimum. Screen your feed for orthophosphate before promising high recovery.

### 3.4 The usable constraint

Scaling is avoided if the fluid's residence time in the supersaturated zone is short relative to the induction time. With vessel length $L$ and crossflow velocity $u$, and a safety factor $\lambda$ (use $\lambda \ge 10$; the tail element, not the average, sets the risk):

$$t_A\!\left(S_{a,w}(Y,J_w,\text{pH}),\,C_a\right) \;\ge\; \lambda\,\tau_{res}, \qquad \tau_{res} \approx \frac{L}{u} \;(\text{plus recycle loops in CCRO/batch})$$

For once-through RO $\tau_{res}$ is seconds to a minute and almost any dose passes — which is why the *real* constraint is the deposited-crystal residence time on the membrane and in dead zones, not the bulk. For CCRO/batch RO, closed-circuit recycle makes $\tau_{res}$ the *batch* time and this constraint becomes genuinely binding, which is the correct way to couple Stage-5 recovery pushes to chemistry. Warsinger et al.'s salinity-cycling result (Water Research, 2018, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.watres.2018.01.060) is the same physics read forward: batch RO's periodic desupersaturation resets the induction clock before nuclei reach critical size.

## 4. Setting the antiscalant dose

### 4.1 The state of practice is a 3–10× overdose

Two independent field programs converge on this. Mangal, Yangali-Quintanilla, Salinas-Rodríguez, Dusseldorp, Blankert, Kemperman, Schippers, Kennedy & van der Meer (*Journal of Membrane Science* 650:120717, 2022, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.memsci.2022.120717; full method in the 2023 thesis above) ran a feedback dose-search algorithm — co-developed with **Grundfos A/S** as a smart digital dosing pump — on two groundwater RO pilots:

| Pilot | Feed | Supplier dose | Algorithm dose | Reduction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kamerik, NL (6+2+1 PV, 3× Hydranautics ESPA2-LD-4040 each, 80 % recovery) | anaerobic GW, Ca 115, HCO₃ 400, TDS 750–800 mg/L, 2.1 mg/L PO₄, 8.6 mg/L DOC, 12 °C | 2.0 mg/L | **0.2 mg/L** | ~90 % |
| Brabrand, DK (3 PV × 2 FilmTec BW30-4040, recirculation to 80 % recovery, 2.0 m³/h feed, 1.2 m³/h permeate) | aerobic GW, Ca 155, HCO₃ 412, SO₄ 80, TDS 793 mg/L, 12 °C | 4.5 mg/L | **0.6 mg/L** | ~87 % |

The 0.6 mg/L setpoint held NDP flat for **54 days**. Headline: **85–90 % antiscalant reduction** versus supplier recommendation.

Independently, Rahardianto, Gu, Khan & Plumlee (*AWWA Water Science* 2:e1196, 2020, https://doi.org/10.1002/aws2.1196) applied a sequential dose step-down on a pilot RO at a **100-MGD** municipal wastewater reclamation facility, using the **RO-Spotlight™** real-time membrane surface imaging system to detect scale onset directly rather than waiting for flux decline, and cut antiscalant use **26 %** while reaching the optimum far faster than conventional flux-tracking pilots.

### 4.2 The algorithm (implementable as written)

The control loop is an extremum-seeking/step-down search on a *normalized* performance signal, not on raw pressure. Mangal's monitoring equations (from Hydranautics practice and ASTM D4516-00):

$$K_w = \frac{Q_p}{NDP\cdot A_M}\cdot\frac{1}{TCF_t}, \qquad TCF = \exp\!\left[2700\left(\frac{1}{298}-\frac{1}{273+T}\right)\right]$$
$$NDP = P_f - \frac{\Delta P_{fc}}{2} - P_p - \pi_{fc} + \pi_p, \qquad NDP_T = NDP\times TCF$$

Procedure:
1. Start at the supplier's recommended dose.
2. Step the dose **down** on a fixed schedule and watch $d(NDP_T)/dt$ of the **last stage** (or, better, the last element / an external scale-guard). Kamerik: 9 steps of 0.2 mg/L, 12 h dwell each. Brabrand: steps of 0.5 mg/L, 24 h dwell, floor 0.6 mg/L.
3. The dose at which $\Delta NDP/\Delta t$ turns positive is the **underdose**; the lowest dose holding $NDP_T$ flat is the **optimum**.
4. On detected rise, step back **up** and hold.

Terminology the thesis is careful about, and which you should encode: *underdose* < *optimum* ≤ *safe dose* < *overdose*.

**Why the loop is safe** — the non-obvious proof-of-principle. Underdosing during the search causes real scaling; the algorithm only works if that scaling is *arrestable*. Mangal verified this three ways at LSI 2.0–2.2 synthetic concentrate (Ca²⁺ 675–765 mg/L, HCO₃⁻ 2350–2670 mg/L, pH 7.6, 20 °C, 3 L stirred reactor at 150 rpm, induction time = time to a 0.03-unit pH drop):
- Jar: no-AS induction time ~15 min; 2.0 mg/L (feed-equivalent) extended it to >1 week; adding 2.0 mg/L *after* a 0.1-unit pH drop **halted** further pH decline for >1 week — even with fresh seed crystals present.
- Lab RO (Sterlitech SEPA cell, 140 cm² coupon from Hydranautics ESPA2-LD-4040, once-through, 0.10–0.12 m/s, 0.5–0.7 % recovery): permeability fell at 0.2 mg/L, and restoring 2.0 mg/L after a **27 % permeability drop** stopped further decline.
- Pilot: NDP rose immediately at 0 mg/L during three windows (30–33 h, 90–93 h, 150–153 h) and the rise ceased each time dose was restored.

**Overdose is a real failure mode, not just a cost.** At 5.0 mg/L feed dose (33.3 mg/L in concentrate, Ca²⁺ 765 mg/L) permeability fell *faster than at 0.2 mg/L*. SEM showed an amorphous film rather than calcite cubes; ESI-TOF and ICP-MS at TZW DVGW identified **calcium phosphonate**. The Danish pilot reproduced this at 6.0 mg/L constant dose (concentrate Ca 775 mg/L, AS 30 mg/L) with a slow NDP rise that 0.6 mg/L did not produce. So the dose–performance curve is **U-shaped**, and a naive "more antiscalant is safer" heuristic is wrong at high recovery where concentrate Ca is high.

**Known weaknesses to fix in your implementation.** The published decision tree ratchets up and never re-tries downward, so a transient upset (the Danish unit jumped to 6.0 mg/L on operational disturbances, then settled at 1.2 mg/L when 0.6 was sufficient) permanently inflates the setpoint. Mangal's own recommendation is an HMI warning on every dose increase plus operator adjudication of "scaling vs. disturbance." Better engineering: (a) add a disturbance detector (CUSUM on $NDP_T$ residual against a fouling-free model, gated on stable feed T, conductivity, flux) before accepting an increase; (b) schedule periodic re-descent with finer steps (0.1–0.2 mg/L) after a stable period; (c) run the search on an **external scale-guard** — an extra element fed with last-stage concentrate so it scales *first* (van de Lisdonk et al.) — so the search never risks the production train.

### 4.3 Closed-loop dose verification: tagged antiscalants

Feedback on NDP tells you whether the dose is *sufficient*; it does not tell you the dose actually delivered (pump slip, dilution, degradation). Fluorescent-tagged antiscalants close that loop. Ecolab/Nalco's **3D TRASAR for membranes** is the commercial instance — the vendor describes a dispense→measure-KPI→ECOLAB3D cloud→metrics→dashboard loop (https://www.ecolab.com/nalco-water/offerings/3d-trasar-technology-for-membranes) but publishes no tracer chemistry or quantified savings, so treat vendor numbers as unverified. The open-literature counterpart is Popov and co-workers' naphthalimide-tagged aminophosphonate **ADMP-F** benchmarked against tagged bisphosphonate HEDP-F and tagged polyacrylate PAA-F1 (*International Journal of Molecular Sciences* 24:3087, 2023, https://www.mdpi.com/1422-0067/24/4/3087), reporting **80–100 % scale-inhibition efficacy at 1–3 mg/L** in NACE-protocol tests plus confocal 3D visualization of where the inhibitor ends up on the deposit. Practical use: dose the tagged product at a fixed ratio to the working product, measure fluorescence inline on feed and concentrate, and you get (i) delivered-dose verification, (ii) a concentrate/feed ratio that independently estimates system recovery, and (iii) an early flag when antiscalant is being consumed by the deposit rather than passing through.

### 4.4 Where ML actually fits — and where it does not

Be honest about the literature: targeted searches of OpenAlex for `antiscalant AND machine learning` and `scaling index prediction neural network desalination brine` return essentially **zero on-topic works** (queried August 2026 via https://api.openalex.org/works). There is no published ML antiscalant-dose controller. What exists, and what a practitioner should build on, is:

1. **Model-free feedback search** (§4.2) — already field-proven, needs no ML.
2. **ML surrogates of the thermodynamics inside an optimizer** (§5) — proven, high value.
3. **A learned residual on the kinetics** — fit $t_{0A}$/$k_s$ from the plant's own scale-guard history so the induction model tracks feedwater drift. This is the genuinely open, publishable slot.

## 5. Equation-oriented optimization with scaling constraints (WaterTAP)

### 5.1 Surrogate formulation (Amusat et al., FOCAPD 2024)

Objective, over decision vector $d$ = (Na₂CO₃ dose, acid dose, water recovery, RO pressure):

$$\min_d LCOW = \frac{CRF\cdot C_{cap} + C_{op}\;[\$/\text{yr}]}{V_{treated}\;[\text{m}^3/\text{yr}]}, \qquad CRF = 0.1$$

subject to mechanistic equality constraints (RO, pumps, ERD per Atia et al. 2023), operational inequalities (inlet crossflow ≤ 25 cm/s, salt rejection ≥ 98 %, RO-1 pressure ≤ 85 bar, HPRO 85–300 bar), and the scaling constraints

$$\widehat{ST}_s(d) \le 1 \quad \forall s\in\{\text{CaSO}_4,\ \text{CaCO}_3,\ \text{CaSO}_4\!\cdot\!2\text{H}_2\text{O}\}$$

Solved with **IPOPT + HSL MA27** inside WaterTAP/IDAES/Pyomo.

Chemistry enters as **PySMO radial-basis-function surrogates** trained on **OLI Studio** equilibrium data swept over the decision ranges (brackish: Na₂CO₃ 0–750 mg/L, pressure 10–110 bar, recovery 50–90 %, CO₂ 0–300, H₂SO₄ 0–150, HCl 0–150 mg/L; seawater: Na₂CO₃ 0–1200 mg/L, pressure 50–300 bar, recovery 50–87 %, acids 0–50 mg/L). Reported accuracy: softening pH and CaCO₃ **R² ≈ 1.00**, max abs error 0.03 pH and 7.59 mg/L; post-acidification pH R² = 0.99 on **100 training points**, ±0.05 pH; scaling-tendency surrogates **>99.2 % classification accuracy** on scaling/non-scaling — the right metric, since the optimizer only needs the feasible/infeasible boundary, not a regression score. Softening used 13-term RBFs.

Results worth carrying into the master report:
- CO₂ is the most expensive acid in both waters: **up to 49 % costlier than H₂SO₄ for brackish** in the no-softening regime, **11.5 %** for seawater (5 % at high recovery). CO₂ needs >3× the HCl dose (weak acid) and forces a *lower* target pH because it adds carbonate.
- Brackish acid demand is **4–6× seawater** for HCl/H₂SO₄ and **7–10×** for CO₂, because of the brackish feed's bicarbonate buffering (385 vs 140 mg/L).
- **H₂SO₄ is not always cheapest.** It adds sulfate, which drives gypsum, forcing **2–41 % additional calcium removal** in softening; in the 66–76 % recovery window HCl wins on total cost. Above 76 % H₂SO₄ wins again on purity/price (93 % purity, $0.12/kg vs HCl 30 % purity, $0.17/kg; CO₂ 99.5 %, $0.24/kg + 0.11 kWh/kg; Na₂CO₃ $0.19/kg). Capital: Na₂CO₃ $12\,985x^{0.5901}$ ($x$ = lb/day); H₂SO₄/HCl $-0.0029x^2+48.434x+22\,648$ ($x$ = gpd), all CEPCI-adjusted to 2018 $.
- Safety inverts the cost ranking: HCl needs the largest 10-day on-site storage (low purity), H₂SO₄ the smallest; CO₂ is non-corrosive and safest.
- **Antiscalants enter this framework by relaxing the RHS of the ST constraint above 1.0** — the authors state this explicitly. That single line is the bridge between §3 and §5: the kinetic model of §3.2 tells you *how far* above 1.0 you may go for a given dose and residence time.

### 5.2 Direct thermodynamics via implicit functions (Reaktoro-PSE, 2026)

Akkor et al. replace surrogates with an **implicit-function / external grey-box model (EGBM)** formulation: Reaktoro solves the equilibrium problem externally and returns outputs *and their derivatives* to Pyomo, so IPOPT sees a reduced-space NLP. This removes the surrogate retraining burden (the prior neural-network route of Dudchenko & Amusat needed **~2 million PHREEQC samples**) and lets you add chemicals without regenerating data. Degrees of freedom: lime, soda ash, HCl and H₂SO₄ doses, RO membrane areas, crossflow velocities, RO pressures. Four waters: standard seawater, USBR brackish, and two USGS brackish (hardness ~500 and ~1500 mg CaCO₃/L).

The result that matters for the playbook is the **four-regime structure**:
- **Regime 1** — acid only, controls carbonate scaling: up to **74 % (seawater), 56 % (USBR), 59 % (USGS-1)**.
- **Regime 2** — *the optimizer manipulates hydraulics, not chemistry*: it lowers average permeate flux and raises crossflow velocity to cut concentration polarization and hence wall gypsum SI (74–75 % SW, 56–64 % USBR, 59–64 % USGS-1). Cost rises via extra membrane area and pressure loss, and acid dose *falls*. **This is the coupling the master report is missing** — flux and crossflow are scaling-control variables, not just energy variables.
- **Regime 3** — lime only; calcium removal is carbonate-limited to <10 %, so it covers a narrow band (75–76 % SW, 64–74 % USBR, 64–68 % USGS-1).
- **Regime 4** — soda ash added; softening dominates cost. USGS Case 2 (hardness 1501 mg/L) sits in Regime 4 at *all* recoveries, and its LCOW actually *falls* with recovery because osmotic pressure rises <10 bar across the range.

Cost anchors for pretreatment from Lugo, Mejía-Saucedo, Senanayake, Stoll, Sitterley, Wang, Kota, Kuravi, Fthenakis, Kurup & Xu (*Water* 17:708, 2025, https://docs.nlr.gov/docs/fy25osti/93400.pdf), a WaterTAP TEA on Kay Bailey Hutchison BWRO brine (TDS 11 000 mg/L, plant recovery 82.5 %, targeting +60 % recovery on the brine): chemical softening **LCOW $0.96/m³** with **6.3 kWh/m³** and 1.4 kg CO₂-eq/m³; coagulation $0.05–1.2/m³ (chemicals 88–93 % of OPEX); unit chemical costs $0.24/kg Ca(OH)₂ (95 %), $0.28/kg Na₂CO₃ (98 %), $0.59/kg NaOH (50 %), sludge disposal $0.03/kg. Ranking by LCOW: IX > CS > EC > CC; best combination EC + IX. Where non-carbonate hardness dominates, **85–95 % of CS operating cost is chemicals** — which is precisely why buying kinetic headroom with a few tenths of a mg/L of antiscalant beats buying it with soda ash.

## 6. Biofouling: the constraint no saturation index can see

### 6.1 Detection and the early-warning device

Biofouling shows up as feed-channel pressure drop long before it shows up in flux, and it is a **feed-spacer** phenomenon (Vrouwenvelder et al., *Water Research*, 2009, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.watres.2008.11.019). The **Membrane Fouling Simulator (MFS)** is a flat-sheet side-stream cell with the same channel height, spacer and crossflow as the spiral element, run on the plant's feed and monitored on $\Delta p$, optionally with optical coherence tomography.

Huisman, Franco-Clavijo, Vrouwenvelder & Blankert (KAUST/TU Delft, *Journal of Membrane Science* 668:121400, 2023, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.memsci.2023.121400; OA copy https://repository.tudelft.nl/islandora/object/uuid%3A992b640e-f19a-4113-a99b-1dd97432bf0a/datastream/OBJ/download) give the cleanest quantitative index:

$$BFI = \frac{1}{t_{\Delta p_{rel}=100\%}}, \qquad \Delta p_{rel}(t) = \frac{\Delta p(t)-\Delta p_0}{\Delta p_0}$$

i.e. the inverse of the time to double the clean-channel pressure drop — dimensionally a **fouling rate / required cleaning frequency**, directly usable as the renewal-reward rate in the CIP-scheduling objective already in file 04. Two findings matter: the index correlates with biofilm volume **independent of applied crossflow velocity**, and $\Delta p$ is better scaled by the velocity at the **perimeter of a spacer cell** than by the average channel velocity — fix your normalization or cross-plant comparisons are meaningless.

Feedwater biofouling-potential proxies (Al-Juboori & Yusaf review, *Membranes* 2:804, 2012, https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0375/2/4/804): ATP, total direct cell count, AOC and biofilm formation rate are *offline* early-warning tools — they flag problem biofilm before any $\Delta p$ rise, but need sampling. AOC targets in the literature: <100 µg/L (LeChevallier), ~50 µg/L (Bradford), <10 µg/L (van der Kooij), with Hijnen et al. reporting a **threshold AOC of ~1 µg/L** for spiral-wound biofouling — below routine assay capability, which is exactly why online $\Delta p$ on an MFS is the practical instrument. Hijnen et al. also established the quantitative link between ATP/carbohydrate and pressure drop that lets you translate biomass to hydraulics. Online flow cytometry is the emerging real-time substitute (*Membranes* 14:185, 2024, https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0375/14/9/185).

Magnitude of the penalty, from the modelled scenario in the npj Clean Water 2022 RO biofouling review (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41545-022-00183-0): at constant 85 % recovery, a **~10-day lag** with negligible pressure change, then feed pressure **+27 %** and brine pressure **+13 %** over 20 days, peak water flux 9 → 7.3 µm/s, and **SEC 0.4 → 0.5 kWh/m³ (+27 %)** with permeate TDS essentially unchanged (rejection 99.72–99.76 %). Salt passage does *not* move — so a diagnostic rule keyed only on rejection will miss biofouling entirely.

### 6.2 Biocide strategy and its hard constraints

- **Free chlorine is not an option on polyamide.** Commercial PA tolerance is **~1000 ppm·h** cumulative and continuous exposure should stay **<0.1 ppm** (Jiang, Li & Ladewig / npj Clean Water 3:1, 2019, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41545-018-0024-8). Degradation is N-chlorination followed by irreversible Orton rearrangement and chain scission; one reported case gained 65 % flux while losing 7 % rejection. Chlorination also fails biologically (spore-formers survive; regrowth on chlorine-generated AOC) and generates DBPs.
- **Dechlorination placement is a control variable.** SMBS:free-chlorine ≈ **3:1**, and moving the SMBS injection point *forward* along the pretreatment line **increased** biofouling — because chlorination upstream of the antiscalant/SMBS creates AOC that feeds the biofilm.
- **Your antiscalant may be feeding the biofilm.** Phosphonate antiscalants supply phosphorus, typically the limiting nutrient; the 2022 npj review states polyphosphonate products "most likely accelerated biofilm formation." This is a second, independent reason to run at the *minimum* effective dose rather than the vendor dose, and a reason to prefer phosphorus-free polycarboxylates on P-limited feeds.
- **DBNPA** (2,2-dibromo-3-nitrilopropionamide) is the standard non-oxidizing shock biocide: compatible with polyamide, highly rejected by RO, field-demonstrated; the stated drawback is cost. Chlorine dioxide is reported "most effective at minimizing biofouling in first-pass RO and NF." Dichloroisocyanurate inactivated biofilm as well as chlorine while preserving flux and rejection, where chlorine-exposed membranes fell from ~99 % to **80 %** rejection. Note the honest gap: **neither major review publishes DBNPA concentrations, contact times or shock frequencies** — that data lives in vendor bulletins and must be established per plant.
- **Shock dosing has an optimizable interior.** The best-quantified recent shock study is in membrane distillation, but the trade-off structure transfers: **120 mg/L Cl₂ for 2 h**, quenched with NaHSO₃ at 2.5:1 molar ratio, deactivated >90 % of feedwater bacteria and cut 110-h flux loss from **49 %→18 % at 45 °C** and **67 %→21 % at 55 °C**, with biofilm **68 %/73 % thinner** and biofilm vapor-transfer resistance falling 3.3×10¹⁰→1.84×10¹⁰ m⁻¹ (45 °C) and 5.1×10¹⁰→2.1×10¹⁰ m⁻¹ (55 °C). At 65 °C the same shock *caused failure* (pore wetting at 90 h, 89 % thinner biofilm but selectivity lost) because chlorination converts NOM to hydrophilic low-MW compounds and heat-stressed cells secrete biosurfactant EPS (npj Clean Water, 2026, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41545-026-00578-3). The generalizable lesson: shock intensity has an optimum, and the failure mode above it is *membrane damage*, not under-treatment — exactly the U-shape seen with antiscalant overdose.
- Chlorinated feed also indirectly reduces scaling: sparser biofilms entrap fewer Ca/Mg salts, so biofouling and scaling are coupled, not independent.

## 7. A coupled formulation for the optimizer

Put §3–§6 together as a single continuous-chemical RTO layer. Decisions $d = (Y, J_w, u, \text{pH}, C_a, \text{shock schedule})$; minimize cost rate:

$$\min_{d}\; \underbrace{c_e\,SEC(Y,J_w)\,Q_p}_{\text{energy}} + \underbrace{c_{AS}\,C_a Q_f}_{\text{antiscalant}} + \underbrace{c_{acid}\,\dot m_{acid}(\text{pH})}_{\text{acid}} + \underbrace{c_{bio}\,\dot m_{bio}}_{\text{biocide}} + \underbrace{\frac{C_{CIP}+C_{repl}}{T_{run}}}_{\text{renewal}}$$

subject to
1. $S_{a,s}^{wall}(Y,J_w,u,\text{pH}) \le S^{max}_s$ — thermodynamic screen (Reaktoro/OLI/PHREEQC or an RBF surrogate with >99 % boundary classification);
2. $t_A(S_{a,s}^{wall}, C_a) \ge \lambda\,\tau_{res}(Y,u)$ — the kinetic constraint from §3.2, which is what *permits* $S^{max}_s > 1$;
3. $C_a^{min} \le C_a \le C_a^{overdose}(\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}_{conc})$ — the U-shape; upper bound from the calcium-phosphonate solubility in the concentrate;
4. $T_{run} \le 1/BFI(\text{AOC}, u, T, \text{biocide})$ — biofouling-limited run length from the MFS index;
5. $\int C_{Cl_2}\,dt \le 1000$ ppm·h over element life; continuous free chlorine < 0.1 ppm;
6. the usual thermodynamic-restriction and hydraulic bounds ($\Delta P \ge \pi_0/(1-Y)$, crossflow ≤ 25 cm/s, ≤85 bar per BWRO element).

Constraint 2 is the piece that was missing. Without it, the optimizer either treats $SI \le 1$ as hard (leaving 5–20 points of recovery unclaimed, since real plants run concentrate LSI +1.8 to +2.5 with antiscalant) or relaxes it with an arbitrary constant credit (and eventually scales the tail). With it, dose and recovery are co-optimized against a physically grounded rate.

## Implementation notes

**Data you need.** (1) Full ionic feed analysis — Na, K, Ca, Mg, Ba, Sr, Cl, SO₄, HCO₃, F, NO₃, **SiO₂, orthophosphate**, Fe/Mn, plus pH, T, TDS, DOC. Orthophosphate and silica are the two that silently cap recovery (Mangal's 2.1 mg/L PO₄ killed high-recovery operation; brackish silica 12–60 mg/L against 100–150 mg/L solubility). (2) Per-stage $P_f$, $\Delta P_{fc}$, $P_p$, $Q_p$, conductivities and T at ≥5-minute resolution — you cannot run the dose search on hourly data because the NDP slope test needs a clean derivative within a 12–24 h dwell. (3) Last-element or scale-guard permeate flow (the whole-stage average dilutes the signal ~3×). (4) Biofouling: an MFS on the RO feed logging $\Delta p$ continuously; quarterly ATP/AOC on feed. (5) Chemical prices and dosing-pump calibration curves; antiscalant certificate of analysis (active %, P content). (6) For validation of overdose limits, concentrate Ca²⁺ and antiscalant residual.

**Math to implement, in order.** (a) Speciation and $SI$ at the **wall of the tail element** — $\beta=\exp(J_w/k)$ with a spacer-corrected Sherwood correlation, $CF=[1-Y(1-R)]/(1-Y)$. (b) The Sagiv closed forms: $n_c$, $t_{0A}$, and the implicit $t_A$; solve $t_A$ by Newton (one variable, monotone). (c) ASTM D4516 normalization + Mangal's $TCF=\exp[2700(1/298-1/(273+T))]$ and $NDP_T$ — every control decision runs on the normalized signal. (d) The dose-search state machine with dwell time, step size, floor, ratchet-guard and CUSUM disturbance gate. (e) $BFI = 1/t_{\Delta p_{rel}=100\%}$ from the MFS. (f) LCOW/cost-rate objective with $CRF$.

**Tool stack (open source).** Python + **Pyomo/IDAES/WaterTAP** for the flowsheet; **Reaktoro** via **Reaktoro-PSE** (https://github.com/watertap-org/reaktoro-pse; replication flowsheets at https://github.com/watertap-org/reaktoro_enabled_watertap) for in-loop chemistry with exact derivatives, or **PySMO** RBF surrogates trained on **PHREEQC** (free) or **OLI** (commercial) sweeps if you need speed; **IPOPT + HSL MA27**; PHREEQC's `pitzer.dat` for sulfates in high-ionic-strength brine. Score scaling-tendency surrogates by *classification accuracy at ST = 1*, not R² — the FOCAPD models hit >99.2 % and that is the number that governs feasibility.

**Build order that de-risks.**
1. Install an **external scale-guard** element on last-stage concentrate plus an **MFS** on the feed. Everything below runs on these two devices, so no experiment ever risks the production train.
2. Reproduce your plant's SI-vs-recovery curve for calcite, gypsum, barite, celestite, silica at the tail wall. Locate the knee. Compare to the FOCAPD pattern (flat then steep at 64–66 %/74 %).
3. Run the **step-down dose search** on the scale-guard, at production recovery, with 12–24 h dwells and 10 % steps. Expect the vendor dose to be 3–10× the optimum (26 %, 87 %, 90 % reductions reported). Stop-loss: abort and restore dose on >5 % normalized permeability loss — the published evidence is that up to 27 % loss was still arrestable, so 5 % is conservative.
4. Fit $k_s$ from the no-antiscalant scale-guard run and $k_a,k_d$ from two doses; you now have $t_A(C_a, S_a)$ for your water and can *predict* the dose for a new recovery target instead of re-searching.
5. Add the kinetic constraint to the WaterTAP model (relax $\widehat{ST}\le 1$ to $\widehat{ST}\le S^{max}$ where $S^{max}$ is whatever $t_A \ge \lambda\tau_{res}$ permits) and re-solve for recovery. Watch for **Regime 2 behaviour** — if the optimizer lowers flux and raises crossflow instead of adding chemical, that is correct and it is telling you CP is your cheapest scaling lever.
6. Only then touch biocide. Establish the MFS baseline $BFI$, trial DBNPA shock at vendor concentration/frequency, and measure $\Delta BFI$. Keep a hard ledger of cumulative oxidant ppm·h.

**Pitfalls, specifically.**
- Computing SI on feed or bulk concentrate instead of tail-element wall — understates the driving force by $\beta$ (typically 1.1–1.4, locally 2–3×).
- Treating antiscalant as a saturation credit. It changes kinetics only; the projection-program display is a convention, not physics.
- Assuming monotone dose–benefit. Calcium-phosphonate precipitation made 5.0 mg/L *worse* than 0.2 mg/L at 765 mg/L concentrate Ca. Bound the dose from above.
- Running the dose search on a feed with orthophosphate or high silica — amorphous calcium phosphate and polymerized silica are not inhibited by phosphonates and the search will never converge.
- Acid choice by unit price. H₂SO₄'s sulfate load forced 2–41 % more calcium removal and made HCl cheaper over a 10-point recovery band.
- Letting the dose-search ratchet ride after a transient — add the disturbance gate and a scheduled re-descent.
- Diagnosing biofouling from salt passage. Rejection stayed 99.72–99.76 % while feed pressure rose 27 %; use feed-channel $\Delta p$.
- Chlorinating ahead of an SMBS point that is too far forward — you manufacture AOC and make biofouling worse.
- Cross-comparing MFS results normalized on average channel velocity instead of spacer-cell perimeter velocity.

**Honest gaps.** No peer-reviewed ML controller for antiscalant dose exists as of this survey; DBNPA shock concentrations/frequencies are not in the open literature; Ecolab's 3D TRASAR tracer mechanism and savings are unpublished; and the Sagiv model is validated only on CaSO₄ with two antiscalant chemistries — $k_a,k_d$ for CaCO₃, BaSO₄ and modern polycarboxylates must be measured, not looked up.
