## 1. Why the disposal term — not SEC — sets the inland recovery optimum

Global context: desalination plants produce ~141.5 million m³/day of brine (~51.7 billion m³/yr), about 50% more brine than product water (Jones, Qadir, van Vliet, Smakhtin & Kang 2019, *Sci. Total Environ.* 657:1343–1356, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2018.12.076). For a coastal SWRO plant, the marginal cost of sending brine to an ocean outfall is a few cents per m³ and the recovery optimum is genuinely an energy/capital trade (research note 04 §1). For an **inland BWRO plant every m³ of concentrate is a liability priced at $0.5–10/m³**, one to three orders of magnitude above the marginal energy cost of squeezing out another m³ of permeate. This inverts the optimization: the recovery decision variable is driven almost entirely by the disposal term until a scaling constraint binds.

Hard evidence that the disposal route dominates plant economics at BWRO scale — Texas Water Development Board survey of nine real plants (Arroyo & Shirazi 2012, TWDB paper 12-06, https://www.twdb.texas.gov/innovativewater/desal/doc/Cost_of_Desalination_in_Texas_rev.pdf):

- Completed Texas BWRO plants (1.2–27.5 MGD, 2,500–4,365 mg/L feed): capital $2.03–3.91 per gallon of installed capacity; O&M $0.53–1.16/kgal; total production cost **$1.09–2.40/kgal = $0.29–0.63/m³** ($357–782/acre-ft).
- The controlled experiment the report accidentally contains: **City of Roscoe** (0.432 MGD, 3,800 mg/L, *surface-water concentrate discharge*) produces water at **$1.47/kgal ($0.39/m³)**, unit capital $4.09/gal; **Fort Hancock WCID** (0.4 MGD, 1,600–2,400 mg/L — *easier* water — but forced to build **evaporation ponds**) lands at **$3.27/kgal ($0.86/m³)**, unit capital $8.44/gal. Same size, same era, lower-TDS feed — and the disposal route alone **more than doubles** both unit capital and total production cost. TWDB states this explicitly: "Construction cost of evaporation ponds increases the unit capital cost and the total production cost."

Who disposes where (survey of 96 U.S. municipal desalting plants; Mickley 2006, *Membrane Concentrate Disposal: Practices and Regulation*, 2nd ed., USBR Desalination & Water Purification Research Report 123, https://www.usbr.gov/research/dwpr/reportpdfs/report123.pdf, Ch. 5): **surface discharge 41%, sewer 31%, deep-well injection 17%, evaporation pond 2%, land application 2%, recycle 2%** (remainder reuse/unknown). The two cheap routes (surface, sewer) cover 72% of plants — and both are exactly the routes that are capacity-limited or regulation-limited for new inland capacity, which is why the MLD/ZLD market exists.

## 2. Disposal-route economics: numbers and the engineering cost models behind them

### 2.1 Planning-level $/m³ by route

The most widely cited per-route unit-cost table is Panagopoulos, Haralambous & Loizidou 2019, "Desalination brine disposal methods and treatment technologies – A review," *Sci. Total Environ.* 693:133545, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2019.07.351 (metadata verified via Semantic Scholar; full text paywalled — ranges below are as tabulated in that review, which draws on Mickley/USBR data, and are reproduced across dozens of citing works):

| Route | Cost ($/m³ of brine) | Practical limits |
|---|---|---|
| Surface water / ocean outfall | **0.05–0.30** | NPDES/whole-effluent-toxicity permits; salinity/density plume limits; no new inland capacity in many basins |
| Sewer discharge (with surcharge) | **0.32–0.66** | POTW TDS/flow caps; increasingly refused for RO concentrate; surcharge escalation risk |
| Deep-well injection (DWI) | **0.54–2.65** | Suitable geology (confined saline aquifer); UIC Class I/V permitting 2–5 yr; seismicity moratoria; economic only at large flows |
| Land application / spray irrigation | **0.74–1.95** | Requires blending to irrigation salinity; land-intensive; climate-limited; small flows only |
| Evaporation pond | **3.28–10.04** | Arid climate, cheap flat land; liner cost dominant; little economy of scale |

Independent cross-checks: a practitioner content survey (DuckDuckGo-indexed industry sources, August 2026) put brine disposal at "$0.50–8/m³" added cost and 5–10% of OPEX for coastal vs. 15–25% for inland plants — consistent with the table. Mickley 2006 (USBR 123, §2.1.8) gives the qualitative ordering and the key structural facts: DWI "minimal cost of a well is high enough that these wells are typically used only with large concentrate flow rates"; evaporation ponds have "little economy of scale"; thermal ZLD "is not typically an economical disposal option… the energy cost is, by far, the major element."

### 2.2 Deep-well injection cost model (implementable)

USBR 123 Ch. 9 (Mickley 2006) provides a worksheet model (Florida conditions, where nearly all municipal concentrate DWI exists) and a 35-case linear regression:

$$C_{cap,DWI}\;[\$] = -288{,}000 + 145{,}900\,d_{tube}\,[\text{in}] + 754\,\cdot\,\text{depth}\,[\text{ft}]$$

Worked example from the report: 16-inch tubing, 3,400 ft well → **$4.61M** regression vs. $4.497M worksheet (2001 dollars; multiply by ~1.7–1.9 ENR escalation for 2025). Cost stack in the worksheet: logging/testing $350k, drilling/reaming $790k, injection tubing $430k, packer $97k, casing $920k, grouting $600k, dual-zone monitoring well $600k, mobilization 20% of well cost ($710k). Diameter has "surprisingly low influence" — drilling/cementing/testing dominate, which is why the well is a step-fixed cost and DWI unit cost collapses with scale: amortizing $4.6M at CRF ≈ 0.094 (20 yr, 7%) over 1 MGD of concentrate (1.38 Mm³/yr) gives **$0.31/m³** + pumping (~0.1–0.2 kWh/m³, low-head pumps per USBR Fig. 11.7) + monitoring; the identical well serving 0.1 MGD costs **>$3/m³**. This nonlinearity is exactly what an optimizer must see: DWI is cheap *per m³* only above roughly 0.5–1 MGD of concentrate. WaterTAP ships a zero-order DWI unit whose capital = pump + pipe (pipe distance in miles, diameter in inches) with electricity registered as a costed flow (`cost_deep_well_injection()`, https://watertap.readthedocs.io/en/latest/technical_reference/unit_models/zero_order_unit_models/deep_well_injection_zo.html).

### 2.3 Evaporation pond cost model (implementable)

Sizing is evaporation-limited: required evaporative area $A = Q_b / e_{net}$ with $e_{net}$ the net (pond-corrected) evaporation rate. 1,000 m³/day of brine at $e_{net}$ = 1 m/yr needs **36.5 ha** — the land intensity is the whole story. USBR 123 Ch. 10 regression (valid 10–100 acres, 2001$):

$$C_{unit}\;[\$/\text{acre}] = 5406 + 465\cdot t_{liner}[\text{mil}] + 1.07\cdot C_{land}[\$/ac] + 0.931\cdot C_{clear}[\$/ac] + 217.5\cdot h_{dike}[\text{ft}]$$
$$A_{tot} = 1.2\,A_{evap}\big(1 + 0.155\,h_{dike}/\sqrt{A_{evap}}\big)$$

Worked example (60-mil liner, $5,000/ac land, $4,000/ac clearing, 8-ft dike, 10 evaporative acres): $45.5k/acre × 13.6 total acres → **$743k capital**. The liner is the dominant capital element in most states. Annualizing a small-plant case — 100 m³/day brine, arid Southwest $e_{net}$ ≈ 1.4 m/yr → ~6.4 evaporative acres, ~$380k (2001$) — gives ≈ **$1.0–1.3/m³ (2001$; $1.8–2.5/m³ today)** before periodic solids dredging, consistent with the low end of the Panagopoulos 3.28–10.04 range once escalation and solids handling are added. Costs scale *linearly* with volume (no economy of scale), so ponds lose to everything else as flows grow — and pond cost is exquisitely sensitive to recovery upstream, which is why Fort Hancock-type plants are the strongest ML/optimization candidates: **every point of recovery removes pond acreage at ~$45k+/acre**.

### 2.4 Thermal ZLD cost model (the ceiling on every treat-vs-dispose decision)

USBR 123 Ch. 12 worksheet, 1 MGD (3,785 m³/d) of RO concentrate, 5% brine-concentrator reject to crystallizer (34.7 gpm), $0.10/kWh, 2001$:

| Element | Value |
|---|---|
| Brine concentrator (falling-film MVC, CaSO₄-seeded slurry) installed capital | **$5.3M** (≈ $1,400 per m³/d) |
| Crystallizer (forced-circulation VC) installed capital | **$2.65M** (≈ $14,000 per m³/d — 10× the BC per unit flow) |
| BC power 3,750 kW → **23.8 kWh/m³ feed**; crystallizer 525 kW on 7.88 m³/h → **66.6 kWh/m³ feed** | |
| Total annualized cost | **$4.14M/yr → $3.00/m³ of concentrate (2001$; ≈ $5–5.5/m³ in 2025$)** |

Regression (valid 0.4–2.0 MGD): $C_{annual}[\$] = -2{,}722{,}800 + 4{,}035{,}700\,Q[\text{MGD}] + 37{,}720\cdot\text{reject}[\%] + 28{,}591{,}000\cdot c_e[\$/kWh]$. These energy figures match the canonical review (Tong & Elimelech 2016, "The Global Rise of Zero Liquid Discharge," *ES&T* 50:6846–6855, https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.6b01000): brine concentrators 20–25 kWh/m³, crystallizers 52–66 kWh/m³, versus 2–6 kWh/m³ for RO. BC recovery is 90–98%, product <10 mg/L TDS, blowdown up to 250,000 mg/L (USBR 123 §12.1). A current vendor-side sanity check: adding crystallization to a 100-gpm membrane-concentration train adds "**$10 to $20 million**" of capital on top of a $1–2M RO system (SAMCO Technologies, https://samcotech.com/cost-treat-brine-reuse-disposal/), and "membrane systems typically cost 5–10 times less than thermal brine concentration evaporators" (Saltworks Technologies 2019, https://www.saltworkstech.com/articles/reverse-osmosis-brine-treatment-minimize-volume-cost/).

The structural conclusion for the optimizer: **the marginal cost of concentrate is a piecewise function** — cheap routes with hard capacity/permit caps ($0.05–0.7/m³), DWI with a step-fixed well ($0.3–3/m³), ponds linear in volume ($2–10/m³), thermal ZLD ($5+/m³, 20–90 kWh/m³). MLD (90–99% recovery, stopping short of the crystallizer) exists because the last few percent of recovery carry the crystallizer's $14k per m³/d capital and 66 kWh/m³ — you buy volume reduction with membranes until the marginal membrane cost meets the disposal price.

## 3. The disposal-cost-aware recovery optimum: formulation

### 3.1 Objective

Per m³ of product, with recovery $Y$, feed-referred costs $c_f$ (pretreatment + feed pumping + chemicals, $/m³ feed), disposal price $c_{dis}$ ($/m³ brine), electricity price $c_e$:

$$\mathrm{LCOW}(Y, \Delta P, \dots) \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{CRF\cdot C_{cap}(Y,\Delta P)}{V_p}}_{\text{capital}} \;+\; \underbrace{c_e\,SEC(Y,\Delta P)}_{\text{energy}} \;+\; \underbrace{c_{dis}\,\frac{1-Y}{Y}}_{\text{disposal}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{c_f}{Y}}_{\text{feed-side}}$$

subject to the thermodynamic restriction $\Delta P \ge \pi_0/(1-Y)$, flux/CP limits, and scaling constraints $SI_{gypsum}, SI_{SiO_2}, LSI \le SI_{max}$ evaluated on the *concentrate* with a Pitzer-based model (research notes 01 §"osmotic pressure" and 04 §1 carry the SEC identity and constraint set; Zhu, Christofides & Cohen 2009, *IECR* 48:6010–6021, http://pdclab.seas.ucla.edu/Publications/AZhu/AZhu_PDChristofides_YCohen_IECR_2009_48_Effect_Thermodynamic_Restriction_Energy_Cost_Optimization_RO_Membrane_Water_Desalination.pdf).

### 3.2 First-order condition and the closed form worth memorizing

At the thermodynamic limit with an ideal ERD, $SEC = \pi_0/(1-Y)$ (in consistent units; 1 bar = 0.0278 kWh/m³). Then

$$\frac{\partial \mathrm{LCOW}}{\partial Y} = \frac{c_e\,\pi_0}{(1-Y)^2} - \frac{c_{dis}+c_f}{Y^2} = 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \boxed{\,Y^* \approx 1 - \sqrt{\frac{c_e\,\pi_0}{c_{dis}+c_f}}\,}\;\;(Y^*\to 1 \text{ limit})$$

Two plug-ins that reproduce industry behavior from first principles:

- **Inland BWRO, 2 g/L feed** ($\pi_0 \approx 1.5$ bar → $c_e\pi_0 = \$0.0029$/m³ at $0.07/kWh), DWI at $c_{dis}+c_f = \$2$/m³: $Y^* \approx 1 - \sqrt{0.0015} \approx$ **96%**. The energy term is *three orders of magnitude* too small to stop you; the true stop is gypsum/silica saturation (at $Y=0.96$ the brine is 25× concentrated and $\pi_b \approx 39$ bar). **For inland BWRO the recovery optimum is disposal- and scaling-limited, never SEC-limited.** Any Stage 5 optimizer that maximizes efficiency without the $c_{dis}(1-Y)/Y$ term will park the plant at the wrong recovery.
- **Coastal SWRO** ($\pi_0 \approx 27$ bar → $c_e\pi_0 = \$0.75\cdot0.07 = \$0.0525$/m³), outfall at $c_{dis}+c_f \approx \$0.15$/m³: $(1-Y)^2 = 0.35 \Rightarrow Y^* \approx$ **41%** — i.e., the observed 40–50% SWRO recovery falls straight out of the same formula when disposal is nearly free.

Sensitivity of the disposal term itself: raising inland recovery 80%→90% saves $c_{dis}[(0.25)-(0.111)] = 0.139\,c_{dis}$ per m³ of product — **$0.28/m³ at a $2/m³ DWI price**, which is larger than most published AI energy-optimization savings on BWRO (cf. research 04's consolidated table). Recovery is the highest-value knob the optimizer owns at inland plants.

### 3.3 The treat-vs-dispose master decision rule (MLD/ZLD chain design)

Generalize by letting the plant *buy down* brine volume with a concentration technology $t$ (high-recovery RO, LSRRO, OARO, CFRO, MVC) at marginal levelized cost $\mathrm{LCOW}_t(c_{feed,t}, Y_t)$:

$$\min_{\text{chain}} \; \sum_t \mathrm{LCOW}_t \cdot V_t \;+\; c_{dis}\,V_{b,final} \;-\; p_w \,V_{w,recovered}$$

Concentrate further only while marginal concentration cost < ($c_{dis}$ avoided + water value $p_w$). This is precisely the query the Atia et al. (2023) cost-optimal LCOW/SEC maps answer (Section 4): e.g., concentrating a 70 g/L brine at 55% recovery costs $1.89/m³ of recovered water — decisively worthwhile against a $3–10/m³ pond or ZLD backstop, marginal against a $0.5/m³ sewer. The chain optimum for most inland MLD projects lands at primary RO → softening → UHP-RO/LSRRO to 120–250 g/L → small pond/crystallizer for the residue, because the membrane segment's $0.7–7/m³ undercuts thermal's $5+/m³ up to ~125 g/L feed (Atia et al. 2023, below).

## 4. LSRRO and OARO: cost-optimization state of the art

### 4.1 Atia et al. 2023 — the implementable reference formulation

Atia, Allen, Young, Knueven & Bartholomew, "Cost optimization of low-salt-rejection reverse osmosis," *Desalination* 551:116407 (2023); accepted manuscript openly at OSTI: https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/1958140. All numbers below extracted from that full text.

**Process.** LSRRO = one conventional RO stage followed by $n-1$ low-salt-rejection (LSR) stages; each LSR stage's *saline permeate is recycled to the previous stage's inlet*, so brine concentrates monotonically down the cascade. Deliberate salt passage keeps transmembrane osmotic pressure low enough to run at ≤65 bar with (potentially) commercial NF or oxidant-treated RO modules — versus OARO/COMRO which need counterflow high-pressure modules that don't exist commercially at scale (LSRRO origin: Wang, Deshmukh, Du & Elimelech 2020, *Water Research* 170:115317, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.watres.2019.115317).

**Optimization model.** NLP minimizing LCOW, built in WaterTAP (Pyomo/IDAES, equation-oriented), solved with **IPOPT + MA27**; stage count is a discrete outer loop (compare solutions at n = 1…8). Decision variables *per stage*: membrane area, inlet Reynolds number, inlet pressure, and — the paper's novelty — the **salt permeability coefficient B of each LSR stage independently** (first stage's B fixed). Governing equations (their Eqs. 1–12): solution–diffusion $J_w = \rho_w A(\Delta p - \Delta\pi)$, $J_s = B\,\Delta C$; 1-D finite-difference feed-side mass/momentum balances; film-model CP $C_{f,m} = C_{f,b}e^{J_w/\rho_w k_f} - \frac{\rho_w J_s}{J_w}(e^{J_w/\rho_w k_f}-1)$; friction $p_{l,f} = F_f \rho_f v_f^2/2d_h$ with spacer-channel Sherwood/friction correlations; pump $P = Q\Delta p/\eta$; ERD $P = Q\Delta p\,\eta$ (Pelton/turbocharger form); NaCl property regressions at 25 °C.

**Techno-economic parameters (Table 1).** RO stage: A = 1.5 LMH/bar, B = 0.126 LMH, max 85 bar. LSR stages: A = 5 LMH/bar fixed (NF-like), B optimized, max 65 bar. Permeate ≤ 1,000 ppm. Channel height 1 mm, spacer porosity 85%. Pump η = 75%, PX η = 80%. Capital: RO modules **$30/m²**, LSR modules **$50/m²**, pumps **$53/(bar·m³/h)** (function of discharge pressure, not Δp — deliberately conservative for booster pumps), ERD **$535/(m³/h)**; total-investment factor 2×equipment. Opex: electricity **$0.07/kWh**, membrane replacement **15%/yr**, MLC 3% of investment/yr. Load factor 90%, CRF 10%.

**Cost-optimal results (Table 2).** Feed 1 kg/s:

| | Case A | Case B | Case C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feed / recovery | 35 g/L, 70% | 70 g/L, 55% | 125 g/L, 35% |
| Optimal stages | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| Final brine | 114.4 g/L | 153.5 g/L | 190.8 g/L |
| Membrane area | 181.6 m² | 367.7 m² | 880.1 m² |
| **SEC (product)** | **3.93 kWh/m³** | **8.38** | **30.39** |
| **LCOW** | **$0.70/m³** | **$1.89/m³** | **$7.41/m³** |
| Electricity share of LCOW | 40% | 31% | 29% |
| Aggregated pumping vs membrane cost | 4.9× | ~3.8× | 3.3× |

Stage-level structure: every stage rides its pressure bound (85/65 bar); optimal B rises down-cascade (13.7 LMH single LSR stage in A; 13.3→25.6 in B; 12.5→39.9 in C); observed salt rejection falls 51.4→20.5%; water flux 14.8–17.9 LMH (LSR), 11.5–14.9 (RO); inlet Re 123–180. Forcing a *single* B across LSR stages (as all prior work did) raises LCOW by 1.1% (B), 5.1% (C), and **28% for the 70 g/L / 75% recovery case** (membrane area ×2.3) — per-stage selectivity optimization is not a refinement, it is the difference between viable and not.

**Sensitivities (Fig. 5–6).** Electricity 7→20 ¢/kWh: LCOW +72/57/52% (A/B/C); at 2.5 ¢/kWh (renewable LCOE): LCOW 0.52/1.51/6.04 $/m³ (−25/−20/−19%). LSR membrane $50→$100/m²: +2/12/19%. RO max pressure 85→125 bar: LCOW −10/−44/−29% with stage-count drops (A→1 stage near 90 bar; B 3→2 near 88 bar; C 4→3 near ~100 bar) — **ultra-high-pressure first stages are the single biggest LSRRO cost lever**. LSR max pressure 65→85 bar: −18% LCOW (B), 3→2 stages; 65→40 bar (today's low-rejection modules): +54%, 3→4 stages, and 40→85 bar nearly halves cost-optimal SEC. Realistic near-term combo ($30/m² but 40 bar): **+38% vs baseline** — pressure rating beats membrane price. Enforcing the TFC polyamide permeability–selectivity tradeoff ($B \propto A^3$) and co-optimizing A,B: LCOW changes only −0.4/−3/−4%, optimal A = 10.5–15.5 LMH/bar.

**Full application map (Fig. 7; 128×128×8 ≈ 131k NLP solves on NREL HPC via mpi4py).** Feed 5–220 g/L, recovery 30–90%, 1–8 stages, NaCl-solubility cap $x \le 0.26$: LCOW spans **$0.32–41.5/m³**, SEC **1.1–171.2 kWh/m³**. Single-stage RO remains cost-optimal up to 65 g/L at 30% recovery, and to ~88% recovery at 5 g/L; beyond that LSRRO takes over.

**Versus competing technologies (Table 4).** LSRRO beats MVC decisively at moderate salinity (68 g/L/75%: **$3.8 vs $10.3/m³**, −63%; 84 g/L: $6.7 vs $7.8, −14%), beats CGMD by 24–31% at 100 g/L, splits with OARO (OARO cheaper at 30–40% recovery and ≥125 g/L; LSRRO cheaper at higher recovery/lower salinity — e.g., 75 g/L/50%: $2.01 vs $2.7, −26%), and loses to VAGMD at 125 g/L/50% (+63%). Verdict: **LSRRO may be cost-competitive below ~125 g/L feed**; above that, evaporative wins on both LSRRO and OARO because cost-optimal SEC blows past MVC's 20–30 kWh/m³. Energy-only comparison agrees: LSRRO consumes less energy than OARO at ≤35 g/L and more at ≥70 g/L (Wang et al. 2021, *ES&T*, https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.1c01638). Caveats the authors flag: pure NaCl (no scaling — no published LSRRO/OARO model yet includes mineral scaling), no pilot demonstration of LSRRO exists, and permeate spec 1,000 ppm (tightening to 500 ppm adds 2–13%).

Concept: Bartholomew, Mey, Arena, Siefert & Mauter 2017, *Desalination* 421:3–11, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.desal.2017.04.012. Cost optimization: Bartholomew, Siefert & Mauter 2018, *ES&T* 52:11813–11821, https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.8b02771 — LCOW **$1.7–8.8/m³** for 75–150 g/L feeds at 30–50% recovery (numbers as compiled in Atia Table 4), sitting between RO ($0.3–1/m³) and MVC ($7.8–10.3/m³).

### 4.3 The implementable path: WaterTAP flowsheets

- **LSRRO flowsheet** (docs: https://watertap.readthedocs.io/en/latest/technical_reference/flowsheets/lsrro.html; code: https://github.com/watertap-org/watertap/tree/main/watertap/flowsheets/lsrro — `lsrro.py` main flowsheet, `multi_sweep.py` for parameter sweeps, `lsrro_ui.py`, tests/). Objective LCOW $=(f_{crf}C_{cap}+C_{op})/(f_{util}Q)$. Configuration switches: `A_case`/`B_case` ∈ {fixed, single_optimum, per-stage}, `AB_tradeoff` (none / equality / inequality $B \ge k A^3$), NaCl solubility cap $x \le 0.2614$ optional, permeate limit default 500 ppm. Defaults: RO A = 4.2e-12 m/Pa·s, B = 3.5e-8 m/s; LSR A ∈ [2.78e-12, 4.2e-11], pressure bounds 10–85 bar (RO) / 10–65 bar (LSR), pump η 0.75, ERD η 0.8. Degrees of freedom you must fix: stage count, system recovery, feed state, channel geometry.
- **OARO flowsheet** (docs: https://watertap.readthedocs.io/en/latest/technical_reference/flowsheets/oaro.html): multi-stage OARO units + pumps/ERDs + final RO polish; defaults OARO A = 1e-12 m/Pa·s, B = 8e-8 m/s, channel 2 mm, spacer porosity 0.75, product ≤500 mg/L.
- Costing blocks register electricity/chemical flows automatically; DWI zero-order unit closes the disposal term (§2.2) so a **single Pyomo model can carry membranes + disposal and find the disposal-aware optimum of §3 directly**.

## 5. Commercial MLD/ZLD landscape — where the sector's optimization money actually goes

**Gradiant — CFRO.** Counterflow RO stages place a controlled brine on the permeate side to cut effective transmembrane osmotic pressure (the same Δπ-suppression trick as LSRRO/OARO, in counterflow), concentrating brine "**up to 250,000 mg/L TDS as NaCl**" — near saturation — using "commercially available components and membranes" at pressures below UHP-RO (Gradiant, https://www.gradiant.com/technologies/counter-flow-reverse-osmosis-cfro/). Secondary-source claims (indexed webinars/whitepapers): ~**half the energy of MVC evaporation** at <1,000 psi (69 bar), and a USBR pilot producing drinking-quality permeate from SWRO brine. Named deployments: a European semiconductor fab and a U.S. resource-recovery mining site (capacities undisclosed). Academic root: split-feed counterflow RO for brine concentration — Bouma & Lienhard 2018, *Desalination* 445:280–291, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.desal.2018.07.011 (DOI verified via Crossref). **SmartOps AI is sold as an optional layer on CFRO plants** (monitoring/prediction ahead of disruption) — i.e., the commercial MLD stack is high-recovery hardware + the same digital twin/ML layer profiled in research note 06 §2.9, whose one verified number remains the IPMVP-audited **up to 5% energy saving** on a >200,000 m³/d SWRO (ENGIE Middle East; https://www.gradiant.com/success-stories/smartops-ai-desal-optimization/) and ">10%" claimed at an Australian project (https://www.gradiant.com/technologies/smartops-ai/). Note: the brief's "Free Flow" branding could **not** be verified anywhere on gradiant.com or the indexed web (site search returns nothing); treat that name as unconfirmed — the verifiable Gradiant MLD/ZLD offering is CFRO (+ Carrier Gas Extraction for the thermal tail) under water-as-a-service commercial structures.

**Saltworks — UHP-RO MLD.** Publishes the most concrete numbers in the space (https://www.saltworkstech.com/articles/minimal-liquid-discharge-with-advanced-reverse-osmosis-paper/ and https://www.saltworkstech.com/articles/reverse-osmosis-brine-treatment-minimize-volume-cost/): **XtremeRO** spiral-wound UHP-RO rated **1,800 psi (120 bar)** concentrates to **130,000 mg/L TDS**; volume-reduction ladder 5×/10×/20×/40×/70× = 80/90/95/97.5/99% recovery; pilot: **99% recovery on 1,800 mg/L cooling-tower blowdown, concentrated to 130 g/L (70× volume reduction)**, enabled by **BrineRefine** chemical softening with a *real-time calcium sensor* driving dosing (a closed-loop measurement-to-dose control problem — the practical ML/control entry point in MLD pretreatment); "membrane systems typically cost 5–10× less than thermal brine concentration evaporators." Their disposal-well article frames the design rule of §3.3: reduce volume incrementally "to balance wastewater treatment economics vs. disposal costs," RO applicable below ~90,000 mg/L TDS (https://www.saltworkstech.com/articles/protect-disposal-wells-capacity-and-cost/).

**Aquatech.** Full thermal+membrane ZLD house: **HERO™** (high-efficiency RO — high-pH operation after hardness/CO₂ removal to suppress silica scaling), evaporation/crystallization (e.g., Thacker Pass lithium), a "Water Island" ZLD recovering **99% of plant wastewater**, "2,000+ installations," and **DesalPro™ AI-Powered Desalination Optimization** as the digital layer (https://www.aquatech.com/technologies/zero-liquid-discharge/). Quantified AI results: none published.

**ML on full-scale ZLD operations** (the one peer-reviewed anchor): spatio-temporal deep learning (ConvLSTM) on a full-scale ZLD facility forecasts RO performance with **R² = 0.960 at 1-day and 0.942 at 7-day horizons** (*ES&T* 2025, https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.est.5c06257; cross-ref research note 01 §benchmarks) — i.e., the forecasting layer needed for scheduling CIP/softening/evaporator load in an MLD train is demonstrated at plant scale. On the optimization side, the AI+NSGA-II BWRO study (Water Research 2025, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0043135425018378; research note 04 §2.2) that moved operation from 15% to 50–70% recovery with >50% energy reduction is best read as a *disposal-volume* result: at 15%→65% recovery the brine-per-product ratio falls from 5.67 to 0.54 m³/m³ — worth ~$10/m³-product at a $2/m³ disposal price, dwarfing the energy saving.

## Implementation notes

**Data you need.** (1) A firm site disposal-cost function, not a guess: sewer surcharge schedule ($/m³ + TDS/BOD riders) or POTW contract; DWI quotes (well capital via the USBR regression as a sanity check, $754/ft is the dominant term; permitting timeline); land price + net pan evaporation (NOAA/NWS evaporation atlas; multiply pan by ~0.7) for ponds; trucking $/m³·km if applicable. (2) Feed ion analysis sufficient for Pitzer speciation (Ca, Mg, SO₄, HCO₃, SiO₂, Ba, Sr) — the recovery ceiling is a scaling computation, not an energy one. (3) Electricity tariff. (4) For LSRRO/CFRO screening: candidate membrane A/B values and pressure ratings (LSR-stage max pressure is the #1 sensitivity; anything rated 40 bar adds ~50% to LCOW vs 65 bar per Atia Fig. 6). (5) For the ML layer: the same historian tags as research 04/07 plus softening-loop analyzers (real-time Ca hardness) if running >90% recovery.

**Math you must implement.** The LCOW(Y) objective of §3.1 with the disposal term $c_{dis}(1-Y)/Y$ and feed term $c_f/Y$; the closed form $Y^* = 1-\sqrt{c_e\pi_0/(c_{dis}+c_f)}$ as a design sanity check; Pitzer-based SI constraints on the concentrate (PHREEQC/pyEQL/Reaktoro per research note 01); the USBR DWI/pond/ZLD regressions (§2.2–2.4, escalate 2001$→today by ~1.7–1.9× ENR) as the $c_{dis}$ backstops; and for the high-recovery chain, the treat-vs-dispose rule of §3.3 evaluated against the Atia LCOW map (their SI publishes all figure data).

**Tools (all open-source).** `pip install watertap` → run `watertap/flowsheets/lsrro/lsrro.py` (IPOPT + MA27/HSL; per-stage `B_case` optimization on; `multi_sweep.py` parallelizes the salinity×recovery×stages map — Atia's 131k solves used plain mpi4py); OARO flowsheet for the counterflow variant; zero-order `deep_well_injection` unit + WaterTAP costing to close the disposal term in the same NLP. PHREEQC (Pitzer DB) for the scaling ceiling. For surrogate-in-optimizer work, train on WaterTAP sweep output and embed via OMLT (research 04 §6) — the LCOW maps are smooth and low-dimensional (feed g/L, recovery, stage count), so a small MLP or GP surrogate reproduces them to <2% and makes the disposal-aware recovery optimization fast enough for daily RTO.

**Build order.** (1) Price every legally available disposal route at your site; if surface/sewer is open and uncapped, stop — no MLD economics exist. (2) Compute the scaling-limited maximum recovery with Pitzer + antiscalant limits; compare to $Y^*$ from the closed form — for inland BWRO $Y^*$ will exceed the scaling limit, telling you the binding constraint. (3) If brine still costs >$1/m³, run the WaterTAP LSRRO/OARO map on your feed to find the marginal concentration cost curve; extend recovery while it's below $c_{dis}+p_w$. (4) Only then size the residual route (pond/crystallizer) — its cost is now linear in the small residual volume. (5) Add the ML layer where it has demonstrated value: softening-dose closed loop (Saltworks-style Ca analyzer), 1–7 day performance forecasting (ConvLSTM, R²≈0.94–0.96), and train-dispatch/setpoint RTO (verified ~5%, claimed ~10%) — but recognize from §3.2 that a single recovery-point correction at $2/m³ disposal is worth more than all three.

**Pitfalls.** Do not use literature $/m³ disposal ranges in the objective when a site quote is obtainable — the ranges span 5× and the optimum is sensitive to it. Do not optimize recovery without the concentrate-side SI constraints (the optimizer will happily gypsum-plate the tail elements; no published LSRRO/OARO optimization yet includes scaling — Atia's own caveat — so add interstage softening costs manually). DWI is a step-fixed cost: model it as binary well-count × regression capital, not $/m³, or the optimizer will fractionally build wells. Evaporation-pond area is hyperbolic in recovery ($A \propto (1-Y)/Y$): report pond-acres-saved per recovery point to make the business case legible. And treat vendor MLD claims at face flow-sheet value but audit the energy: "half of MVC" still means 10–15 kWh/m³ at high concentration factors — check it against the Atia SEC map before believing an LCOW.
