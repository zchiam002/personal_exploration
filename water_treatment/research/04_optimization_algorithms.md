# Mathematical Optimization and Metaheuristics for RO/UF Design and Operation

Research note 04 — compiled 2026-08-01. Scope: (a) design-time superstructure/MINLP and multi-objective optimization of RO networks; (b) operation-time real-time optimization (RTO), tariff-aware scheduling, renewable coupling, and batch/CCRO optimization; (c) cleaning (CIP) and membrane-replacement scheduling as optimization problems; (d) surrogate-based optimization with ML models embedded in solvers; (e) solver ecosystems (Pyomo/IDAES/WaterTAP, GAMS, MILP/MINLP solvers) and reported savings.

## 1. The embedded process model (what every optimizer sits on)

Every formulation below wraps the same core physics. For a spiral-wound element, the solution–diffusion model with concentration polarization (CP):

$$J_w = A\,\big(\Delta P - \sigma\,\Delta\pi_m\big), \qquad J_s = B\,(c_m - c_p)$$

$$\frac{c_m}{c_b} = \exp\!\left(\frac{J_w}{k}\right) \quad \text{(film-model CP)}, \qquad \pi \approx i\,c\,R\,T/M_w \ \ (\text{van't Hoff; } \pi_{sw}\!\approx\!0.75\text{–}0.8\ \text{bar per g/L NaCl})$$

with $A$ = water permeability (m/s·Pa), $B$ = salt permeability (m/s), $k$ = mass-transfer coefficient from a Sherwood correlation (e.g., $Sh = 0.065\,Re^{0.875}Sc^{0.25}$ for spacer-filled channels), axial pressure drop $\Delta P_{ax} \propto \rho v^2 f(Re)$, plus stage-wise mass and salt balances $Q_f = Q_p + Q_b$, $Q_f c_f = Q_p c_p + Q_b c_b$, and recovery $Y = Q_p/Q_f$. Fouling enters as a time-decaying permeability $A(t) = A_0 e^{-t/\tau}$ or a resistance-in-series term. These constraints are smooth but nonconvex (bilinear flow×concentration products, exponentials), which is why the field splits between deterministic NLP/MINLP (GAMS/Pyomo + IPOPT/DICOPT/BARON) and population metaheuristics (GA/PSO/NSGA-II) that treat the simulator as a black box.

The single most important operational relation is the **specific energy consumption (SEC)** identity of Zhu, Christofides & Cohen (UCLA, 2009, IECR 48:6010–6021, https://pubs.acs.org/iecred/article-abstract/48/13/6010/1216210/ ; PDF at http://pdclab.seas.ucla.edu/Publications/AZhu/AZhu_PDChristofides_YCohen_IECR_2009_48_Effect_Thermodynamic_Restriction_Energy_Cost_Optimization_RO_Membrane_Water_Desalination.pdf). For a single-stage plant with pump efficiency $\eta_p$ and an ERD of efficiency $\eta_E$ returning brine pressure to the feed:

$$SEC = \frac{\Delta P}{\eta_p Y}\Big(1 - \eta_E\,(1-Y)\Big)$$

The **thermodynamic restriction** (cross-flow limit) requires the applied pressure to at least equal the exit-brine osmotic pressure: $\Delta P \ge \pi_0/(1-Y)$ for a feed osmotic pressure $\pi_0$. Substituting the limiting pressure gives the normalized SEC at the thermodynamic limit:

$$\frac{SEC_{tr}}{\pi_0} = \frac{1-\eta_E(1-Y)}{Y\,(1-Y)}$$

Without an ERD ($\eta_E=0$) this is minimized at $Y^\*=0.5$ with $SEC_{tr}=4\pi_0$ — the classic "optimal recovery is 50%" result; with an ideal ERD ($\eta_E=1$) it becomes $\pi_0/(1-Y)$, monotonically increasing in $Y$, so energy-optimal recovery shifts **down** and the true optimum is set by the trade-off against membrane capital, pretreatment and brine-disposal cost. Every RTO scheme in §3 is, at bottom, tracking the minimizer of this curve as $\pi_0(T, S)$ and permeability drift move it. The companion paper (Zhu, Christofides & Cohen, 2009, "Energy consumption optimization … under feed salinity fluctuation", http://pdclab.seas.ucla.edu/Publications/AZhu/AZhu_PDChristofides_YCohen_IECR_2009_48_Energy_Cost_Optimization_Reverse_Osmosis_Membrane_Water_Desalination_Feed_Salinity_Fluctuation.pdf) extends this to time-varying salinity and time-of-use energy prices, showing the optimal policy front-loads production into low-price/low-salinity windows.

## 2. Design-time optimization

### 2.1 Superstructure MINLP of RO networks

The seminal formulation is **El-Halwagi (1992)**, "Synthesis of reverse-osmosis networks for waste reduction," AIChE Journal 38:1185–1198: a state-space superstructure with four boxes — a pressurization/depressurization stream-distribution box, a matching box for pumps/turbines, an RO stream-distribution box, and an RO matching box — whose combinatorics encode every splitting/mixing/bypass/recycle and every stream-to-unit assignment; the result is an MINLP (summarized in the search record via https://pubs.acs.org/doi/abs/10.1021/ie071316j). **Voros, Maroulis & Marinos-Kouris (1996)**, "Optimization of reverse osmosis networks for seawater desalination," Computers & Chemical Engineering (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/0098135496000683) simplified the state space to two-box networks and solved for stage/pass layouts. **Lu et al. (2007)**, J. Membrane Science (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376738806007071) posed the practical designer's version: minimize total annualized cost over feed concentration and product-spec scenarios with membrane-type selection as discrete decisions. **Saif, Elkamel & Pritzker (2008)** achieved deterministic **global** optimization of the RO network MINLP for wastewater via branch-and-bound with convex relaxations (IECR, https://pubs.acs.org/doi/abs/10.1021/ie071316j). A 2019 extension formulates an **MINLP under time-variant operation constraints** — the network must remain feasible across seasonal temperature/salinity states, coupling one design with multiple operating scenarios (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/337457201_An_MINLP_Model_for_Reverse_Osmosis_Network_Design_under_Time_Variant_Operation_Constraints).

A canonical statement of the design MINLP:

$$\min_{y, x} \; TAC = CRF\cdot\Big(\sum_i c_{mod} n_{i} + C_{PV} + C_{pump}(\dot W) + C_{ERD}\Big) + c_e \int SEC\,dV + C_{chem} + r_{repl}\,C_{memb}$$

subject to: element-level flux/balance equations (§1) for every candidate stage/pass; interconnection balances with binary $y_{jk}\in\{0,1\}$ selecting stream–unit matches (big-M linking $Q_{jk} \le M y_{jk}$); bounds on lead-element flux (≤ ~30–35 LMH), minimum concentrate flow per vessel, max element recovery (~15%), vessel ΔP (< ~4 bar), applied pressure ≤ 83 bar (SWRO elements); product constraints $c_p \le c_p^{max}$ (e.g., 500 mg/L TDS) and $c_{B} \le$ 0.5–1 mg/L boron; ERD selection (pressure exchanger vs Pelton) as discrete alternatives, with brine recycled only downstream of the ERD (constraint noted in the MINLP literature above). Typical costing uses a capital recovery factor $CRF = i(1+i)^n/((1+i)^n-1)$ and a membrane replacement rate $r_{repl}\approx$ 10–20 %/yr.

Two design-space results worth hard-coding as priors:

- **Split-partial second pass (SPSP)**: collecting low-TDS front-element permeate directly to product and treating only rear permeate in the second pass reduced RO pumping energy by **≈7.0%** and shrinks second-pass capital, while meeting boron/TDS specs by tuning the front/back split ratio (DuPont/FilmTec technical paper, https://membranes.com/wp-content/uploads/Documents/Technical-Papers/Application/SWRO/186_6892.pdf ; also Desalination & Water Treatment 13:1–3, 2010, https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.5004/dwt.2010.989 and IDA paper https://ida.memberclicks.net/assets/docs/943.1.pdf).
- **Boron-driven superstructures**: a Computers & Chemical Engineering (2022) superstructure study of RO fed by **decarbonated, high-pH seawater** under boron restrictions found single-pass operation becomes feasible and saves **0.41 kWh/m³** versus conventional two-pass designs (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0098135422003611). Boron rejection rises with pH because borate B(OH)₄⁻ is charged and larger than boric acid, so pass-2 pH (8.8–10.5) is itself a design variable.

### 2.2 Multi-objective optimization: NSGA-II, ε-constraint, lexicographic

**Guria, Bhattacharya & Gupta (2005)**, Computers & Chemical Engineering (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0098135405001298) is the reference application of NSGA variants (NSGA-II, jumping-gene adaptations) to RO desalination units: objectives = maximize permeate throughput, minimize permeate concentration and cost, with membrane area, pressure, and flow as decision variables. NSGA-II mechanics to implement: non-dominated sorting ($O(MN^2)$), crowding-distance diversity preservation, binary tournament, SBX crossover + polynomial mutation; typical settings population 100–200, 200–500 generations.

Deterministic alternatives to evolutionary MOO for RO networks: **lexicographic optimization + augmented ε-constraint (AUGMECON)** applied to RO network design (Desalination, 2014, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0011916413005109) — solve $\min f_1 - \delta \sum_k s_k/r_k$ s.t. $f_k + s_k = \varepsilon_k$, sweeping $\varepsilon_k$ over a payoff table built lexicographically; this yields only Pareto-efficient points and avoids the weighted-sum method's inability to reach nonconvex Pareto segments.

Recent state of the art combines both: the **comprehensive superstructure MOO** of ACS I&EC Research (2024, https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.iecr.4c03467) embeds stages, passes, ERDs, and recycles in one model and solves with **NSGA-II** over four KPIs — water recovery, specific water cost, SEC, CO₂ emissions — mapping their Pareto interactions. On the brackish side, an **AI + NSGA-II framework** (Water Research, 2025, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0043135425018378) reports moving BWRO from fixed-point operation at 15% recovery to optimized regions at **50–70% recovery** with **>50% energy reduction** through integrated tuning of flow, pressure and temperature. For renewable-powered RO, NSGA-II is used to co-minimize LCOE and LCOW while reducing grid dependence (MDPI Water 17(13):1981, 2025, https://doi.org/10.3390/w17131981).

### 2.3 Single-objective metaheuristics: GA and PSO

- **Species-conserving genetic algorithm (SCGA)** on a spiral-wound RO model for dimethylphenol removal: optimizing membrane length, width, and feed-channel height raised rejection by 2.51% (to 98.72%) **and cut SEC by ≈20.6%** versus the stock module (Membranes/PMC, 2021, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8399129/).
- **Rapid-convergent PSO for multiscale SWRO design** with high-permeance membranes (Communications Engineering / Nature, 2024, https://www.nature.com/articles/s44172-024-00289-y) couples module-scale hydrodynamics to system-scale design in one PSO loop; PSO velocity/position updates to implement: $v_i \leftarrow \omega v_i + c_1 r_1 (p_i - x_i) + c_2 r_2 (g - x_i)$, $x_i \leftarrow x_i + v_i$, with inertia $\omega$ annealed 0.9→0.4, $c_1=c_2\approx2$, swarm 30–50.
- A comparative review of AI methods for industrial membrane processes catalogs GA, PSO, Harmony Search and their multi-objective versions (NSGA-II, MOPSO, MOHS) applied to RO design and fouling mitigation (Springer Earth Systems and Environment, 2021, https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41748-021-00220-x).

Rule of thumb from this literature: use deterministic MINLP (DICOPT/SBB/BARON in GAMS; Pyomo+Mindtpy) when the model is algebraic and you need constraint feasibility guarantees; use NSGA-II/PSO when the evaluator is a simulator (WAVE, TorayDS, CFD, or a plant digital twin) and gradients are unavailable.

## 3. Operation-time optimization

### 3.1 Real-time optimization of setpoints (pressure/recovery) under drifting feed

The two-layer architecture that has actually been **field-tested** is described in "Real-Time Energy Optimal Control of Two-Stage Reverse Osmosis Desalination" (MDPI Water 17(16):2363, 2025, https://doi.org/10.3390/w17162363): a supervisory layer solves the SEC model of §1 — accounting for pump efficiency curves, physical constraints, and measured feed salinity/temperature — for the **energy-optimal first-stage recovery, overall recovery, and current membrane permeability** at the target production rate; a regulatory layer with three PI loops tracks feed flow, stage-1 pressure, and stage-2 pressure. Field tests demonstrated robust simultaneous control of the dynamically coupled variables at the energy optimum. This is the modern descendant of the UCLA optimization-based control line (Bartman, Zhu, Christofides & Cohen — "Minimizing energy consumption in RO membrane desalination using optimization-based control," https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229316944 ; MPC of feed-flow reversal for scale control, J. Process Control 2009, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959152408001182).

The RTO problem to solve every 15–60 min, as feed temperature/salinity and fouling drift:

$$\min_{\Delta P_1, \Delta P_2, Y_1, Y} \; SEC(\Delta P_i, Y_i; \pi_0(T,S), A(t), \eta_p(q), \eta_E) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad Q_p = Q_p^{target},\; c_p \le c_p^{max},\; \Delta P \ge \frac{\pi_0}{1-Y},\; J_w \le J_w^{max}$$

**Commercially reported results.** Synauta's ML "setpoints-as-a-service" (recovery manipulation via three setpoints) achieved **18% instantaneous and 9.7% average energy savings over a 6-month trial** at a Western Australia SWRO plant (Smart Water Magazine, https://smartwatermagazine.com/news/synauta/machine-learning-delivers-energy-savings-desalination), claims **up to 15% energy and up to 25% CIP-chemical savings** (Alberta Innovates, https://albertainnovates.ca/news/digital-innovation-machine-learning-for-optimizing-reverse-osmosis/), and **up to 5% ISO-IPMVP-verified energy savings at a large Middle East SWRO** (Global Water Awards 2022, https://globalwaterawards.com/2022-breakthrough-technology-company-of-the-year/). Estimated OPEX impact: >$3.5M/yr for a mega-plant like Taweelah, ~$15M/yr across Australia's six large plants. ACCIONA's ACRRO + "Insight" pairs differential-equation rack optimization with a continuously adjusting real-time ML layer (Smart Water Magazine, https://smartwatermagazine.com/news/smart-water-magazine/when-plant-learns-run-itself-reinforcement-learning-agents-desalination).

**Plant-scale MILP-over-ANN RTO (H2Oaks case).** The neural-network superstructure optimization of the San Antonio H2Oaks BWRO plant (Membranes/PMC, 2022, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8879670/) is the most completely documented implementation: 3-stage plant (2 primary trains of 4 parallel flows + 2-flow concentrator; Dow FilmTec BW30-400/34; max recovery 90%). ANN surrogates trained on **14,542 hourly SCADA records (Jan 2017–mid 2018, 70/15/15 split)**: an ERD ΔP model (1 input, one 3-node ReLU hidden layer, R²=0.9983) and a permeate-concentration model (5 inputs: $Q_f$, $WR_1$, $WR_2$, $WR_3$, $c_{p,t-1}$; one 3-node ReLU layer, R²=0.895); retentate pressure closed by linear correlations $P_{r} = a P_f + b$ (R²>0.99). The linearized SEC objective $SEC^\* = \frac{1}{4\eta_1}\sum P_{f,1,j} + \frac{1}{10\eta_1}(\sum P_{f,3,j} - \tfrac12\sum P_{r,2,j}) - \frac{\eta_2}{20}\sum \Delta P_{ERD,j}$ (η₁=0.74, η₂=0.8) with ReLU networks exactly reformulated to **MILP** via binary big-M encoding, solved in **CPLEX (MATLAB)**. Results: optimized annual RO energy 1,392.9 vs 1,516.9 actual (≈**8% below** actual high-service pump consumption); Pareto SEC–recovery curve 0.2455 kWh/m³ @ 50% recovery, 0.3941 @ 70%, 0.5072 @ 85%, linearized as $SEC = 0.753\,WR - 0.1326$ (R²=0.998) — i.e., recovery 50→85% costs 2.07× the energy, quantifying the water-energy trade the operator can schedule against.

### 3.2 Time-of-use tariffs, load shifting, renewable coupling

**Seminal formulation:** Ghobeity & Mitsos (2010), "Optimal time-dependent operation of seawater reverse osmosis," Desalination 263:76–88 — an MINLP minimizing daily electricity cost for a fixed daily production, with pressure-exchanger ERDs, VFD part-load efficiency curves, CP, temperature effects, and fouling in the model; decision profile = time-varying feed pressure/flow (found via the ResearchGate record https://www.researchgate.net/publication/282424099 and https://www.researchgate.net/scientific-contributions/Amin-Ghobeity-82683145). The general scheduling MILP that has become standard:

$$\min \sum_t \Big( p_t^{buy} E_t^{grid} - p_t^{sell} E_t^{exp} \Big) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad V_{t+1} = V_t + \Delta t \sum_i q_{i,t} - D_t,\; V^{min}\!\le\!V_t\!\le\!V^{max},\; q_{i,t} \in \{0\} \cup [q_i^{min}, q_i^{max}]\, u_{i,t},\; \text{ramp/min-up-down on } u_{i,t}$$

i.e., trains/pumps as semicontinuous units, product storage as the flexibility battery, binaries for unit commitment.

- **MILP demand-side management with PV** for a desalinated-water supply system (SWRO + pumping + reservoirs + PV) in the agricultural industry minimizes daily (purchased electricity − PV sales) and prescribes train/pump schedules (Journal of Cleaner Production, 2021, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S095965262104289X).
- A tariff-optimization case study reports **rescheduling ~60% of daily load into the 23:00–06:00 off-peak window** yields substantial annual savings (Springer, 2026, https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-11358-0_20); a companion IWA paper builds a **digital twin to optimize electricity cost under a time-based tariff** (J. Water Reuse & Desalination 15(3):509, 2025, https://doi.org/10.2166/wrd.2025.039).
- **PV-SWRO for the Middle East**: variable plant operation plus time-varying water conveyance raises the PV share of load to **72%** (Desalination, 2025, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0011916425001213).
- **Renewable-colocated dispatch (arXiv 2026)**: "Optimal Scheduling/Dispatch of Electricity and Water in Renewable-Colocated Desalination" (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.02243) maximizes $\Pi = \pi^w(w_h+w_r) - P_{net-meter} - C_h$ with RO conversion $\alpha_r = 166.67$ m³/MWh (6 kWh/m³ incl. intake/conveyance), RO turndown to 0, net metering at $\pi^+=\$270$/MWh import, $\pi^-=\$100$/MWh export, 50 MW PV (Najran, Saudi Arabia hourly data), RO capacity 8,333 m³/h. Optimal joint dispatch earns **$119,180/day vs $112,850 (passive-thermal benchmark) and $99,872 (max-RO heuristic)** — a 5.6–19% profit improvement — and reduces to a **four-threshold policy** on the renewable/price state computable offline; the marginal value of RO electricity ($\approx f_r'\pi^w = \$167$/MWh at $1/m³ water) sets the import/self-sufficient/export mode boundaries.
- Flexible-operation economics caveat: a Southern California brackish case study finds water-cost reductions materialize **only** when tariffs are aggressive and the facility is **designed** for flexibility (oversized trains, parallel positive-displacement pumps for turndown, storage) (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/402900057) — flexibility is a *design-time* decision consumed at *operation time*. NREL's "Optimizing Desalination Operations for Energy Flexibility" covers the same ground for US tariff structures (https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/2474837).

### 3.3 Batch and semi-batch (CCRO) optimization

Thermodynamics: continuous RO must apply exit-brine pressure to the **entire** feed; batch processes ramp pressure with instantaneous concentration, approaching the reversible limit $SEC_{ideal} = -\pi_0 \ln(1-Y)/Y$. Warsinger, Tow, Nayar, Maswadeh & Lienhard (MIT, 2016, Water Research) model **energy savings up to 37% for CCRO (semi-batch) and 64% for true batch RO** relative to continuous single-stage RO across recoveries and salinities (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/308627314 ; companion https://www.researchgate.net/publication/308958227). CCRO gives up some of the batch advantage to entropy of mixing recirculated brine with fresh feed.

Commercial CCRO (Desalitech, acquired by DuPont): recovery **up to 98%, typically 90–95%**, single-stage hydraulics, and CIP frequency cut from ~monthly (conventional) to ~**every 6 months** (DuPont, https://www.dupont.com/knowledge/rethink-reverse-osmosis-ro.html ; Salt Separation Services, https://saltsep.co.uk/closed-circuit-reverse-osmosis). Pilot validation: Orange County Water District's potable-reuse CCRO pilot raised RO recovery **from 85% to 92%** over a 2-year test at the GWRS (Desalination, 2021, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0011916421003714).

Optimization layers demonstrated on CCRO:

- **Autonomous online optimization via extremum-seeking control (ESC)** (Colorado School of Mines, Water Research X, 2024, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11648806/): model-free, batch-to-batch ESC perturbs the **upper conductivity limit** that terminates each closed-circuit phase (dither: sinusoid, 0.15 mS/cm amplitude, 5-cycle period; high-pass filter isolates the gradient; bumpless transfer on cost changes). Objective $J = SEC + \alpha\,SBV$ (specific brine volume) with constraints 2.8–25 mS/cm and TMP limits, on a 7.9 m² BWRO element pilot with dual VFD pumps (permeate 2.4/1.2 L/min filtration/drain, recirculation 50 L/min). Results: **≈6.5–6.8% improvement** in the cost metric after disturbances (10× brine-cost weight change; +50% feed NaCl), converging in 70 cycles (~35 h) and 150 cycles (~85 h) respectively — with only flow/pressure/conductivity/power sensors. This is the cleanest published template for "RTO without a model."
- **MPC for CCRO** optimizes permeate and recirculation flow setpoint sequences over the batch horizon subject to equipment and cycle-time constraints (IWA Water Supply 25(4):727, 2025, https://iwaponline.com/ws/article/25/4/727/107850/), and a DOE/OSTI report covers energy-efficient CCRO process control (https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/3002293). Batch-RO trajectory optimization under a time-dependent pressure profile is modeled in MDPI Membranes 11(3):173 (2021, https://doi.org/10.3390/membranes11030173).

## 4. Cleaning and maintenance scheduling as optimization

### 4.1 When to CIP

Industry heuristic (the baseline any optimizer must beat): trigger CIP at **10% normalized-flux decline, 5–10% salt-passage increase, or 15% differential-pressure rise** vs. the commissioning baseline, computed on normalized data (ASTM D4516-type normalization) (Daneshline CIP guide, https://en.daneshline.com/membrane-cleaning-procedures/ ; Loyalmem 2026 guide, https://loyalmem.com/ro-membrane-cleaning-guide/).

The optimization view treats CIP as a renewal-reward problem: choose trigger threshold $\theta$ (or times $t_k$) to minimize the long-run cost rate

$$\min_\theta \; \frac{C_{CIP} + \int_0^{T(\theta)} c_e\,SEC(t)\,Q_p(t)\,dt + C_{repl}\cdot\mathbb{1}[\text{irreversible}]}{\int_0^{T(\theta)} Q_p(t)\,dt}$$

where $T(\theta)$ is the run length until the trigger and fouling dynamics $A(t)$ come from a forecast model. The strongest recent result: **long-sequence time-series forecasting (LSTF) deep-learning models + reinforcement-learning simulation of CIP policies** on an industrial RO plant (Desalination, 2025, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0011916425006691) found that raising the pressure threshold for CIP (cleaning later but at higher-value moments, accounting for chemical and replacement costs) and performing **CIP twice at the highest pressure threshold cut RO operating cost by 16.13% while extending operating time by 139.53%** vs. the plant's incumbent strategy. Zondervan & Roffel-type optimal control extends this to the cleaning cycle itself — optimal profiles of cleaning-chemical flux and concentration vs. time to minimize cleaning cost/duration (survey in https://pubs.sciepub.com/ijefm/9/1/2/index.html).

### 4.2 UF backwash scheduling

For UF pretreatment the decision variables are backwash interval, duration, flux, and air assistance; the objective is net water production $NWP = (V_{filtered} - V_{backwash})/t_{cycle}$ net of energy:

- **Stochastic dynamic programming over a data-driven fouling forecast** outperformed fixed-interval backwashing on a pilot UF system — lower cost and lower terminal membrane resistance (J. Membrane Science, 2021, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376738820310413).
- **Response-surface optimization of air-assisted backwash** for seawater UF mapped backwash interval (30–90 min), flow rate, and duration to specific-flux decline and NWP via polynomial models; fouling was controlled primarily by interval (MDPI Membranes/PMC, 2020, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7231395/). Pilot studies report season-dependent optima, e.g., 65 min (dry) vs 55 min (wet) intervals (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2214714424005804); engineering-scale analysis of backwash settings for water reuse in https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2214714423002805. Optimal control of physical backwash strategy generally: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376738817319166.

### 4.3 Membrane replacement timing

Replacement enters design MINLPs as an annualized replacement-rate term, but the *scheduling* problem is a stopping problem: replace when the marginal cost of continued operation (rising SEC at fixed production, rising CIP frequency, salt-passage-driven blending losses) exceeds the annualized replacement cost. Early dynamic-optimization treatment: one-stage RO with fouling (Comput. Chem. Eng., 1992, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/037673889287029W). Design-stage MINLPs with **flexibility constraints** size the system so it can meet spec as performance declines, making intervention timing an economic rather than feasibility decision (see the fouling-aware optimal design & operation MINLP of Chem. Eng. J., 2011, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1385894711004864, which minimizes total annualized cost with thermodynamic, technical, and flexibility constraints, and schedules maintenance on economic criteria). Industry practice: 3–5 year element life in industrial service, condition-based rather than calendar-based (https://xjyco.com/how-often-to-replace-reverse-osmosis-membrane/). The LSTF+RL framework of §4.1 explicitly includes replacement cost in the RL reward, which is what pushed it toward fewer, later CIPs.

## 5. Surrogate-based optimization (ML models inside optimizers)

Four patterns appear in the literature:

1. **Exact MILP embedding of ReLU networks.** A trained ReLU network is a piecewise-linear function; each neuron $y = \max(0, w^Tx+b)$ becomes $y - s = w^Tx + b$, $y \le M z$, $s \le M(1-z)$, $y,s \ge 0$, $z \in \{0,1\}$. The H2Oaks study (§3.1) did this by hand in CPLEX; the general tool is **OMLT** (Ceccon et al., Imperial College, JMLR 23, 2022, https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume23/22-0277/22-0277.pdf ; code https://github.com/cog-imperial/OMLT), which auto-translates Keras/ONNX networks and gradient-boosted trees into Pyomo blocks with selectable formulations — ReLU big-M (Anderson et al. 2020), complementarity, and partition formulations (example network: 248/308/428 constraints respectively) plus full-space vs reduced-space smooth encodings. This turns "optimize plant setpoints over an ML plant model" into a MILP/MINLP solvable by Gurobi/CPLEX/BARON.
2. **Kriging / Gaussian-process Bayesian optimization.** GP posterior $\hat f(x) \sim \mathcal N(\mu(x), \sigma^2(x))$; sample next at $\arg\max EI(x) = (\mu - f^\*)\Phi(z) + \sigma\phi(z)$, $z = (\mu-f^\*)/\sigma$. Used for RO membrane/material and process-condition optimization where each evaluation is an experiment or an expensive simulation (review: "Machine Learning in Membrane Design: From Property Prediction to AI-Guided Optimization," Nano Letters, 2024, https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acs.nanolett.3c05137 ; RO synthesis/optimization ML review, MDPI Materials 18(4):840, 2025, https://doi.org/10.3390/ma18040840). ANN surrogates of CFD-scale RO hydrodynamics cut evaluation from hours of multiphysics solve to seconds, enabling nested design loops (noted in the PSO multiscale study, https://www.nature.com/articles/s44172-024-00289-y).
3. **Trust-region methods with surrogates.** The IDAES platform ships a trust-region-filter (TRF) solver that embeds black-box/simulation units inside an equation-oriented Pyomo flowsheet, rebuilding local surrogates each iteration with convergence safeguards — the rigorous way to use ML surrogates without losing feasibility guarantees; IDAES also provides surrogate trainers (ALAMO, PySMO polynomial/RBF/Kriging, Keras via OMLT). A 2025 benchmark of surrogate-model optimization strategies (Comput. Chem. Eng., https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0098135425002030) compares these families on pooling-type problems.
4. **Reduced/linearized physics surrogates.** The H2Oaks study's linear $P_r$–$P_f$ correlations and linear SEC objective show that for RTO, aggressively simplified surrogates (R² 0.895 on permeate concentration) can be "sufficiently accurate for energy optimization" — accuracy budget should be set by the optimum's sensitivity, not by prediction benchmarks.

## 6. Solver ecosystems

- **Pyomo / IDAES / WaterTAP (NAWI, open-source, Python)**: WaterTAP (https://www.nawihub.org/knowledge/watertap/ ; docs https://watertap.readthedocs.io) provides equation-oriented 0-D/1-D RO unit models (solution-diffusion + CP + friction), pumps, ERDs, property packages (NaCl, seawater), and a costing framework minimizing **LCOW** $= (f_{crf}\,C_{cap,tot} + C_{op,tot})/(f_{util}\,Q)$; NLPs solved with IPOPT, MINLPs via MindtPy/Bonmin, with IDAES diagnostics for scaling/degeneracy. Reference flowsheets include multi-stage RO with ERD, **LSRRO** (pressure bounds 10–85 bar RO / 10–65 bar LSR stages, permeate ≤500 ppm, NaCl mass-fraction ≤0.2614 solubility cap, pump η=0.75, ERD η=0.8; docs https://watertap.readthedocs.io/en/stable/technical_reference/flowsheets/lsrro.html) — the companion paper is Atia, Allen, Young, Knueven & Bartholomew, "Cost optimization of low-salt-rejection reverse osmosis," Desalination 551:116407 (2023, OSTI https://www.osti.gov/pages/servlets/purl/1958140) — and **OARO** flowsheets for hypersaline brines (https://watertap.readthedocs.io/en/stable/technical_reference/flowsheets/oaro.html). WaterTAP has been used for cost-optimal pH control and antiscalant selection against mineral scaling at high recovery (ACS ES&T Engineering, 2024, https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acsestengg.3c00537 ; NETL FOCAPD paper https://netl.doe.gov/projects/files/CostoptimalselectionpHcontrolmineralscalingpreventionhirecoveryreverseosmosisdesalination_071024.pdf). WaterTAP3 layers data-driven models of >50 treatment technologies for screening (same NAWI hub page).
- **GAMS**: the historical home of RO network MINLP (El-Halwagi, Voros, Lu, Saif) — solvers DICOPT (outer approximation), SBB (branch & bound), BARON/ANTIGONE (global). Use when you need deterministic global optima on modest-size networks.
- **MILP solvers** (CPLEX, Gurobi, HiGHS/CBC open-source): pump/train scheduling under tariffs (§3.2), ReLU-embedded RTO (§3.1/§5).
- **Metaheuristic libraries**: pymoo / DEAP / Platypus (NSGA-II, MOPSO), PySwarms; **BO stacks**: BoTorch/Ax, scikit-optimize, GPyOpt.
- **MATLAB** remains common in the control literature (H2Oaks CPLEX interface; MPC studies).

## Implementation notes

**Data you need.** (1) SCADA historian at ≥hourly resolution: feed/permeate/concentrate flows, pressures (per stage), conductivities, temperature, pump power/VFD frequency — the H2Oaks benchmark used 14,542 hourly rows (~18 months) and got usable surrogates with 3-neuron networks; a year of data covering seasonal temperature swing is the practical minimum. (2) Membrane projection data (A, B, ΔP correlations) from vendor tools (WAVE/TorayDS/LG Q+) or fitted from commissioning tests. (3) Normalized performance trends (ASTM D4516) for fouling/CIP work. (4) Tariff schedule and, for renewables, hourly generation profiles (PVGIS/NSRDB). (5) Cost data: $/kWh, membrane element cost (~$500–800 BWRO, ~$600–1,000 SWRO), CIP chemical cost per event, replacement rate 10–20 %/yr.

**Math you must implement.** Solution–diffusion + film CP + axial ΔP per element, chained element-by-element down each vessel (6–8 elements); van't Hoff or Pitzer osmotic pressure; the SEC identity with ERD and the thermodynamic restriction $\Delta P \ge \pi_0/(1-Y)$ as an explicit constraint (optimizers will otherwise exploit infeasible low-pressure corners); CRF-based annualization for design; renewal-reward cost-rate objective for CIP.

**Tool stack (all open-source).** Python + Pyomo + IDAES + WaterTAP for design NLP/MINLP (IPOPT; MindtPy for discrete stage counts; start from the WaterTAP RO-with-ERD and LSRRO flowsheets and their costing blocks); HiGHS or Gurobi for scheduling MILPs; pymoo for NSGA-II over a WAVE-exported or WaterTAP-wrapped simulator; OMLT to embed trained Keras/ONNX surrogates as Pyomo blocks; BoTorch/scikit-optimize for BO when each evaluation is a pilot run.

**Build order that de-risks.** (1) Reproduce the SEC–recovery curve for your plant from the closed-form model; verify the plant's current operating point against it — the gap is your RTO prize (typically 5–15%). (2) Fit ANN/linear surrogates from the historian; validate R² per §3.1 and, critically, check optimizer excursions stay inside the training hull (add box constraints on inputs to the surrogate block). (3) Stand up the RTO as an advisory layer (setpoint recommendations, operator-in-loop) before closing the loop; the two-level architecture (supervisory optimizer + existing PI loops on flow/pressure) of MDPI Water 2025 requires no DCS replacement. (4) Add the tariff MILP only if storage or train-level turndown exists — flexibility not designed in cannot be scheduled (ResearchGate 2025 Southern California study). (5) For CIP optimization, first instrument normalized flux/ΔP/salt passage, then fit a fouling forecaster (even exponential decay fitted per run beats calendar CIP), then optimize the threshold via simulation before touching RL. (6) If model-free is preferred (CCRO or small plants), extremum-seeking on one variable (recovery or conductivity trigger) with a small sinusoidal dither is implementable on a PLC and converged in ~35–85 h in the published pilot.

**Pitfalls.** Nonconvexity: multistart IPOPT (10–50 random initializations) or global solvers on small instances; scale variables (pressures in bar, flows in m³/h) or IPOPT will stall. Surrogate extrapolation is the dominant failure mode of ML-in-the-loop RTO — constrain to the data hull and retrain on drift (fouling shifts the input distribution). MILP big-M values for ReLU embeddings must be tight (compute per-neuron bounds by interval arithmetic) or solve times explode. For batch/CCRO, model the mixing entropy or you will overestimate savings by the difference between the 64% (batch) and 37% (CCRO) bounds. Always benchmark any metaheuristic against the deterministic solution of the same algebraic model — on smooth RO models, IPOPT from a good start typically matches NSGA-II's best point at 100–1000× less compute; metaheuristics earn their keep only on true black-box or multi-objective front-mapping problems.
